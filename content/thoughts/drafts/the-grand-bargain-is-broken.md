# The Grand Bargain Is Broken

In 1421, the architect Filippo Brunelleschi -- the man who had just solved the seemingly impossible problem of building the dome of the Florence Cathedral -- received what is thought to be the first patent in the Western world. The invention was a river transport barge called *Il Badalone*, designed to carry marble from Pisa to Florence up the River Arno "for less money than usual, and with several other benefits." Brunelleschi was [paranoid about competitors](https://knowfuture.wordpress.com/2011/03/09/il-badalone-brunnelleschi-and-the-first-patent/) -- particularly his former collaborator Lorenzo Ghiberti -- stealing the design. Despite the patent, he shared only crude details with a single fellow engineer.

Six years later, Brunelleschi finally built the thing. It [sank on its maiden voyage](https://www.etblaw.com/what-was-the-first-patent/), taking a sizable portion of his personal fortune with it.

The history of patents begins, then, with an inventor who was simultaneously terrified of being copied and unable to make his own invention work. That tension -- between the desire to control an idea and the reality that ideas resist control -- has defined every era since. For more than five centuries, societies have tried to manage it through a mechanism so simple it borders on elegant: the grand bargain. You tell the world exactly how your invention works, in enough detail that anyone skilled in the art could reproduce it. In exchange, the government grants you a temporary monopoly. You get a window of exclusivity; society gets knowledge.

The deal depends on one assumption: that replication is hard enough to make the window meaningful. In 1474, when Venice codified the first patent system, a ten-year monopoly was generous because building anything required physical labor, specialized skills, and material resources. In 1790, when Thomas Jefferson personally examined America's first patent applications -- approving only [57 out of 114](https://www.monticello.org/research-education/thomas-jefferson-encyclopedia/patents/) during his tenure -- the difficulty of reproduction was simply a fact of the physical world.

That assumption held for a remarkably long time. It held through the Industrial Revolution, when steam-powered presses could reproduce printed material but couldn't reproduce machines. It held, mostly, through the digital revolution, when software could be copied instantly but still took teams of engineers months to build from scratch. It held, barely, through the smartphone era, when any feature could be cloned but the cloning still required resources, time, and talent.

It does not hold anymore.

## The Candle and the Machine

Jefferson, characteristically, saw the problem before anyone else. In an [1813 letter to Isaac McPherson](https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/a1_8_8s12.html), he offered what remains the most elegant articulation of why ideas resist ownership:

> "He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me."

The candle metaphor captures something that patent law has always struggled with: ideas are not rivalrous goods. If you take my hammer, I no longer have a hammer. If you take my idea, I still have the idea. The patent system's entire architecture is an attempt to impose scarcity on something that is, by its nature, abundant. Jefferson acknowledged this frankly. He considered patents a "pragmatic concession," not a natural right -- useful policy, perhaps, but working against the grain of how knowledge actually behaves.

The U.S. Constitution reflected this pragmatism. Congress was authorized to grant exclusive rights not to enrich inventors but ["to promote the progress of science and useful arts."](https://www.monticello.org/research-education/thomas-jefferson-encyclopedia/patents/) The inventor's monopoly was a means, not an end. Society was the intended beneficiary.

Patent law has always differed from copyright in this respect. Copyright protects *expression* -- the specific way you wrote a sentence, the particular arrangement of pixels in an image. It arises automatically the moment you create something, costs almost nothing to register, and lasts absurdly long (life plus 70 years in the U.S.). Patents protect *inventions* -- processes, machines, methods, compositions. They require disclosure, demand novelty and non-obviousness, cost [$15,000 to $30,000](https://arapackelaw.com/patents/design-patent-vs-utility-patent/) or more to obtain, take three to five years to process, and expire after 20 years. The fundamental distinction: copyright protects how you expressed an idea; patents protect the idea itself, as embodied in a concrete invention.

If you're trying to protect a product idea -- a feature, an interaction pattern, a design decision -- copyright is categorically the wrong tool. It doesn't cover ideas. That's not a bug; it's a foundational principle established in [*Baker v. Selden*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/101/99/) in 1879 and codified in Section 102(b) of the Copyright Act. Copyright protects the code you wrote, not the concept behind it. Anyone can look at your product, understand the idea, and build their own version with different code. That's legal. That's always been legal.

Patents are the tool designed for the job. They're the mechanism society created to protect the *idea* of a new mousetrap, not just the particular mouse trap sitting on your workbench. When the question is "how do I protect a product concept that anyone could copy," the answer, in theory, is a patent.

In theory.

## An Invention So Valuable as to Be Worthless

In 1794, a Yale-educated tutor named Eli Whitney patented the cotton gin. The device was staggeringly simple -- a rotating drum with wire teeth that separated cotton fibers from their seeds -- and staggeringly transformative. It made short-staple cotton profitable to cultivate at scale, reshaped the economy of the American South, and, catastrophically, [entrenched the institution of slavery](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/cotton-gin-patent) by making plantation agriculture vastly more lucrative.

Whitney made almost no money from any of it.

The cotton gin was so mechanically simple that anyone who saw one could build a copy. A loophole in the [Patent Act of 1793](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Whitney) meant Whitney couldn't collect damages from infringers until the law was amended in 1800. Even after the amendment, lawsuits consumed whatever profits he managed to extract. His cotton gin company went bankrupt in 1797. His patent wasn't fully validated until 1807, the same year Congress refused to renew it when it expired. Whitney's bitter assessment: ["An invention can be so valuable as to be worthless to the inventor."](https://billofrightsinstitute.org/essays/eli-whitney-and-the-cotton-gin)

Whitney's problem was not that patent law didn't exist. He had a patent. His problem was that the invention was too simple to defend. The gap between having a legal right and being able to enforce it was wide enough to swallow his entire business. The patent office said the cotton gin was his; the cotton fields of Georgia said otherwise.

This is the pattern the article needs to hold in its hands. The patent system's promise -- disclose your invention, receive protection -- has always been contingent on enforcement. And enforcement has always been contingent on the economics of copying. When copying is cheap and easy, the patent becomes a piece of paper.

Whitney's story is the original version of a problem that would repeat across centuries, with increasing velocity, until it reached the software industry and became something close to an existential condition.

## Patent Thickets and Flying Machines

The gap between patent-as-right and patent-as-reality widened through the Industrial Revolution, producing spectacles that oscillated between farce and tragedy.

The sewing machine was the smartphone of the 1850s: a complex product requiring multiple patented technologies, no single inventor, and overlapping claims that produced total legal gridlock. The functional sewing machine needed the lockstitch (patented by Elias Howe), the four-motion feed, the vertical needle with horizontal sewing surface, and several other innovations, each held by a different inventor. Every sewing machine manufactured infringed multiple patents, spawning what legal scholar Adam Mossoff described as ["massive litigation by the patent-owners against manufacturers, retailers, and consumers alike."](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1354849)

The resolution was elegant: in 1856, Orlando B. Potter brokered the [Sewing Machine Combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewing_Machine_Combination), the first privately formed patent pool in American history. Howe, Wheeler & Wilson, Singer, and Grover & Baker pooled nine key patents and agreed to cross-license them. At least 24 manufacturers were licensed. The pool lasted until the final patent expired in 1877. The sewing machine wars demonstrated that when multiple patents cover a single product, the system either finds a way to pool them or collapses into unproductive litigation. It was an early, surprisingly civilized solution to a structural problem.

The Wright brothers offered a less civilized version. After receiving their patent for a method of airplane flight control in 1906, the brothers -- through the newly formed Wright Company -- waged aggressive patent warfare, filing [12 major lawsuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers_patent_war) and attempting to secure a monopoly on all U.S. aircraft manufacturing. Their most significant target was Glenn Curtiss, who used ailerons (small flaps on wing tips) instead of the Wrights' wing-warping system. The Wrights argued that ailerons fell within their patent's scope -- a claim that, if accepted, would have given them control over essentially every method of controlling an airplane in flight.

The result was catastrophic in a way that goes beyond legal interest. During the period of intense litigation, the Wrights ["sorely neglected R&D -- so much so that by 1915, when Orville sold the company, their aircraft were derided as dangerous and obsolete."](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/flying-machines/04-patent-wars/) By the time the United States entered World War I in 1917, the government could not find a single American aircraft fit for military service. The birthplace of powered flight had, through patent litigation, fallen behind Europe in aircraft technology. The National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics eventually pressured the industry into forming the [Manufacturer's Aircraft Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers_patent_war) -- a cross-licensing patent pool modeled on the Sewing Machine Combination, sixty years later.

TIME has called the Wrights ["pioneers of what's sometimes known as patent trolling."](https://time.com/4143574/wright-brothers-patent-trolling/) The label is anachronistic but not inaccurate. The brothers who invented powered flight used their patent to prevent the development of better aircraft. The legal monopoly became more valuable to them than the actual innovation, and the system rewarded litigation over invention. This is the nightmare scenario that haunts every discussion of patents: the possibility that the protection mechanism destroys the thing it was designed to protect.

The Bell telephone patent, by contrast, showed what happened when the system worked exactly as designed -- and raised its own uncomfortable questions. On February 14, 1876, attorneys for both Alexander Graham Bell and Elisha Gray submitted telephone-related documents to the U.S. Patent Office [on the same day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Gray_and_Alexander_Bell_telephone_controversy). The patent office ruled that Bell's application arrived first. Bell received his patent on March 7, 1876 -- what has been called the most valuable single patent ever issued. The Bell Telephone Company faced over [600 patent challenges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Telephone_Cases); the Supreme Court upheld Bell's patents in The Telephone Cases (1888). The Bell patent monopoly shaped the American telecommunications industry for the next century. A single filing, a matter of hours in timing, created a monopoly worth more than most countries' GDP. The grand bargain, when it worked, worked almost too well -- concentrating so much value in a single patent that the system looked less like an incentive for invention and more like a lottery ticket.

Meanwhile, Thomas Edison was building something different -- not a single patent fortress but an empire. Edison held [1,093 U.S. patents](https://www.thomasedison.org/edison-patents) and an additional 1,239 foreign patents, totaling 2,332 worldwide. His approach was strategic: inventions weren't isolated devices but systems of interconnected technologies. Electric lighting networks. Phonograph recording systems. Motion picture cameras and projectors. Each patent locked into the others, creating a web of intellectual property that was far more defensible than any single filing.

When Edison felt threatened by George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla's alternating current system, his response went beyond litigation. He staged [public demonstrations where animals were electrocuted](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/edison-vs-westinghouse-a-shocking-rivalry-102146036/) using Westinghouse's AC current. He lobbied to have the electric chair powered by AC, specifically to associate his competitor's technology with death. It was a combination of patent strategy, propaganda, and what would today be called information warfare.

Edison lost anyway. In 1892, J.P. Morgan forced Edison General Electric to switch to AC power and then pushed Edison out of the company he had founded. Edison later admitted he regretted not listening to Tesla. The episode illustrates something the patent system's defenders often downplay: patents don't determine which technology wins. They're one weapon among many -- and not always the decisive one.

## The Strange Case of Software

Everything described so far involves physical inventions. Machines. Processes that transform material. The patent system was designed for a world where inventions had mass, occupied space, and required physical effort to reproduce. Then software happened.

The question of whether software could be patented at all occupied the Supreme Court for decades, producing a trilogy of cases that moved from "probably not" to "well, maybe" to "it depends":

In [*Gottschalk v. Benson* (1972)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottschalk_v._Benson), the Court held that a patent on a method for converting binary-coded decimal numbers to pure binary would "wholly pre-empt the mathematical formula." Mathematical algorithms, standing alone, could not be patented. In [*Parker v. Flook* (1978)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_v._Flook), the Court reinforced this: the mere application of a known formula to a known process was insufficient. But in [*Diamond v. Diehr* (1981)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_v._Diehr), a crucial 5-4 decision, the Court held that a process for curing synthetic rubber -- which *used* a computer program to calculate cure times -- was patentable. The invention, considered as a whole, was a physical process, not a mathematical abstraction.

The line between patentable and unpatentable software was, to put it generously, unclear. It remained unclear for the next seventeen years, until a single Federal Circuit decision blew the doors off.

[*State Street Bank & Trust Co. v. Signature Financial Group* (1998)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Street_Bank_%26_Trust_Co._v._Signature_Financial_Group,_Inc.) held that a data processing system for managing mutual fund investments was patentable. The key holding: business methods were no longer an exception to patent eligibility, as long as they produced a "useful, concrete and tangible result." The practical effect was an explosion. In 1991, software-related patents represented about [20 percent of yearly totals; by 2011, they accounted for about 50 percent](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=8cdd3dd7-1fb3-48dc-a7ba-b6ffb0076e8e). The absolute number rose from roughly 25,000 per year to roughly 125,000 per year. The patent office was inundated with applications for things like methods of doing business on the internet, techniques for displaying information on screens, and ways of processing transactions that, to many observers, seemed less like inventions and more like obvious uses of existing technology.

Amazon's [one-click patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-Click) became the poster child. In September 1999, the USPTO granted Amazon a patent on the ability to purchase an item with a single click by storing payment and shipping information. Amazon immediately sued Barnes & Noble, which offered a similar "Express Lane" feature. A district court [issued an injunction forcing Barnes & Noble to stop](https://www.npr.org/2017/09/06/548819270/amazon-s-1-click-patent-is-about-to-expire-what-s-the-big-deal) -- during the 1999 holiday shopping season. Critics argued the patent gave Amazon an unfair monopoly on "what amounts to little more than an efficient means of using standard technology" and that other websites had to make their shopping systems deliberately less efficient to avoid infringement. The Federal Circuit eventually vacated the injunction, but the patent survived until it expired in 2017. For 18 years, the idea of buying something with one click was, legally speaking, Amazon's property.

The one-click patent was absurd, but it wasn't an outlier. The U.S. Patent and Trademark Office granted patents for a [method of swinging on a swing](https://abovethelaw.com/2017/10/8-of-my-favorite-stupid-patents/) (a lawyer's young son's technique of swinging sideways rather than back and forth), exercising a cat with a laser pointer, and a crustless peanut butter and jelly sandwich. These examples are funny. They also illustrate a system whose quality control had genuinely failed. When you can patent swinging on a swing, the system's claim to protect genuine inventions becomes harder to take seriously.

The patent troll phenomenon was the logical consequence. NTP, Inc. -- a Virginia-based patent holding company founded in 1992 with a portfolio of 50 U.S. patents and no products -- [sued Research in Motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTP,_Inc.) (maker of BlackBerry) for patent infringement in 2001. NTP had never manufactured anything. The case settled with RIM paying NTP $612.5 million -- far greater than the value of the technology in question. By 2011, U.S. businesses were incurring [$29 billion in direct costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent_troll) from patent trolls annually. From 2009 through mid-2013, Apple alone was the defendant in 171 lawsuits brought by non-practicing entities.

The system had drifted far from the grand bargain. The original deal was: inventors disclose useful knowledge, and society rewards them with temporary exclusivity. What emerged instead was a landscape where shell companies accumulated patents like financial instruments, extracted rents from actual innovators, and contributed nothing to the store of public knowledge. The patents weren't swords wielded by inventors defending their work. They were tollbooths on the road, collecting from anyone who passed.

## The Earthquake

On June 19, 2014, the Supreme Court decided [*Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/573/208/). The decision was unanimous. Its consequences were seismic.

Alice Corporation held patents on a computer-implemented scheme for mitigating financial "settlement risk" using a third-party intermediary. The Court held that an abstract idea does not become patent-eligible simply by being implemented on a generic computer. The test was two steps: First, is the claim directed to a patent-ineligible concept -- an abstract idea, a law of nature, a natural phenomenon? If yes, does the claim contain an "inventive concept," something significantly more than the abstract idea itself?

The standard sounds reasonable. In practice, it devastated software patents. Of 76 Federal Circuit decisions dealing with Alice challenges through 2015, [57 invalidated the patents at issue](https://www.eff.org/alice) -- a 75 percent kill rate. The estimated invalidation rate for computer-implemented patents, if challenged, was [82.9 percent](https://btlj.org/2016/03/alice-abstract-ideas-software-related-patents/). Section 101 rejections at the patent office [jumped from 7 percent to 15 percent](https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/OCE-DH_AdjustingtoAlice.pdf) of all denied applications within a year. Of approximately 240,000 U.S. patents related to computer-implemented inventions, an estimated 199,000 would be found invalid if challenged under the Alice framework.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation celebrated. They called it ["Saved by Alice."](https://www.eff.org/alice) For patent trolls, Alice was an extinction-level event. For the thousands of companies defending against frivolous software patent claims, it was a lifeline. For the first time, there was a straightforward mechanism to invalidate the most egregious patents -- the ones that amounted to "do [obvious thing], but on a computer."

But Alice did something else, something with consequences that have taken a decade to fully materialize. It made it dramatically harder to patent *any* software method, including genuinely novel ones. The two-step test offered no bright line between a patentable software invention and an unpatentable abstract idea. A feature that automated a previously manual process? Probably abstract. A novel algorithm for ranking search results? Possibly abstract. An interaction pattern that solved a real user problem in a new way? The patent office wasn't sure, and neither were the courts.

For the software industry, Alice created an inversion. The patent system was supposed to protect inventions. After Alice, the inventions that software companies most wanted to protect -- product features, interaction models, UX innovations, the things that differentiated one product from another -- were precisely the things most likely to be classified as abstract ideas. The tool designed to protect product ideas had been recalibrated to reject them.

This was an inconvenience in 2014. By 2024, it had become something considerably worse.

## The Fast Followers

Before examining what AI changed, it's worth understanding what it accelerated. The tech industry was already running on a copy-first model. The legal gap left by Alice had practical consequences, but they were manageable because copying still required time, teams, and engineering effort. The protection wasn't legal; it was practical. The moat was the difficulty of building things.

In August 2016, Facebook launched Instagram Stories -- a [near-exact copy of Snapchat Stories](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/03/09/facebook-copies-snapchat-examples.html). The feature was so transparently borrowed that Snapchat's 2017 IPO filing acknowledged Instagram Stories "largely mimics our Stories feature." Within months of launch, Snapchat's growth [slowed 82 percent](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/03/09/facebook-copies-snapchat-examples.html). Facebook eventually added Stories to all four of its core apps -- Facebook, Messenger, WhatsApp, and Instagram. In February 2025, Snap CEO Evan Spiegel [mocked Mark Zuckerberg on LinkedIn](https://fortune.com/2025/02/06/snap-ceo-evan-spiegel-mocks-mark-zuckerberg-meta-copycat-features/), calling himself "the VP of Product at Meta" because of how many features they'd replicated.

Spiegel's joke landed because everyone recognized the dynamic. TikTok's short-form video was copied by Instagram (Reels), YouTube (Shorts), and Snapchat (Spotlight). LinkedIn added Stories. Twitter added Fleets, then killed them. Tinder's swipe-right mechanic was reproduced by dozens of dating apps, a social commerce startup, and at least one app for adopting shelter dogs. When Match Group sued Bumble in 2018 for copying Tinder's "world-changing, card swipe-based, mutual opt-in premise," Bumble's response achieved a kind of rhetorical perfection: ["We swipe left on you. We swipe left on your multiple attempts to buy us, copy us, and, now, to intimidate us."](https://www.npr.org/2018/10/30/660006488/the-tinder-bumble-feud-dating-apps-fight-over-who-owns-the-swipe)

The Bumble response captured something real. In the social media industry, copying wasn't a scandal -- it was the business model. Features migrated across platforms like birds across borders: indifferent to the lines drawn on maps, following their own logic. Nobody sued successfully. Nobody needed to. The protection that mattered wasn't a patent filing in Alexandria, Virginia. It was distribution. Facebook could copy Snapchat Stories because Facebook had two billion users. Snapchat couldn't copy its way to two billion users.

The same dynamic played out with design patents. Apple sued Samsung and eventually [extracted $533 million](https://wolfgreenfield.com/ip-alerts/533-million-apple-v-samsung-verdict-highlights-importance-of-design-patents) for design patents covering, among other things, a rectangular front face with rounded corners and a grid of colorful icons on a black screen. The verdict proved that design patents had teeth. But it also raised the obvious question: is the shape of a smartphone -- a rectangular slab with rounded corners, which is essentially the only practical form factor for a touchscreen device -- something that should be protectable at all? Apple's design patents covered the specific ornamental treatment of a phone. They didn't cover the *concept* of a touchscreen smartphone. Samsung paid half a billion dollars, redesigned its phones slightly, and kept competing.

This is the limitation that runs through every form of IP protection for software. A design patent protects [*this specific visual treatment*](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1223&context=njtip) of a swipe gesture, not the idea of swiping to make a choice. Trade dress protects against a product that looks confusingly similar to yours, not against a product that [does the same thing with a different visual treatment](https://ipo.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/PracticalApproachtoProtectionofGUIinUS.pdf). Copyright protects the code you wrote, not the function the code performs. And utility patents, post-Alice, struggle to cover software concepts at all.

The tech industry adapted to this reality not by seeking better legal protections but by investing in things that couldn't be copied: network effects, proprietary data, embedded workflows, brand trust. As [Greylock Partners noted](https://greylock.com/greymatter/the-new-new-moats/): "Technological advantages have a short half-life and aren't very defensible anymore -- if you're first to market, competition will copy your technology or develop it themselves before long." Andreessen Horowitz went further, arguing that even ["data moats are rarely durable"](https://a16z.com/the-empty-promise-of-data-moats/) -- that data advantages erode as competitors collect similar information.

The modern smartphone, covered by an estimated [250,000 active patents](https://www.techdirt.com/articles/20121017/10480520734/there-are-250000-active-patents-that-impact-s) -- one in six of all active U.S. patents -- represented the logical endpoint of this system. Companies maintained vast patent portfolios primarily for defensive purposes, a form of mutual assured destruction. The patents weren't for asserting rights; they were for [cross-licensing negotiations](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/the-sky-is-not-falling-navigating-the-smartphone-patent-thicket-55490). Google's $12.5 billion acquisition of Motorola in 2012 was primarily for its patent portfolio -- a $12.5 billion insurance policy. Patents in tech had become shields, not swords. The actual competitive advantages -- speed, network effects, data, brand -- were unrelated to IP law.

The system was working, in the sense that the industry was innovating rapidly despite weak IP protections. The fast-follower problem was real but survivable. You could copy a feature, but you couldn't copy an installed base. You could clone an app, but you couldn't clone a community. The window between innovation and replication was narrowing, but it still existed. A few months, maybe. A quarter or two. Enough time to establish the feature, iterate on it, build the audience.

Then the window closed.

## The Collapse

Generative AI compressed the replication cycle from months to hours. What previously required a team of engineers working for weeks -- understanding a feature, designing it, implementing it, testing it -- could now be accomplished by a single developer describing the feature to an AI coding assistant and iterating on the output over a weekend. The practical difficulty of copying, which had been the tech industry's real protection for two decades, evaporated.

Two developments converged. Alice made software patents dramatically harder to obtain, with an [82.9 percent invalidation rate](https://btlj.org/2016/03/alice-abstract-ideas-software-related-patents/) for challenged computer-implemented patents. Generative AI made software features trivially replicable. The legal mechanism that could have protected product ideas was weakened at exactly the moment the threat it was supposed to guard against was amplified by orders of magnitude.

The protection gap that had always existed in software IP -- the space between what the law covers and what actually has value -- went from a manageable inconvenience to something that looked, increasingly, like an existential problem. Consider the landscape:

Copyright protects the specific code you wrote, not the idea behind it. A competitor can look at your product, understand the concept, and have an AI generate a functionally identical implementation using entirely different code. Legal.

Utility patents, post-Alice, struggle to cover software concepts. An interaction pattern, a product feature, a UX innovation -- these are likely to be classified as "abstract ideas" that can't be patented. And even if you could obtain a patent, it takes three to five years. The feature you're trying to protect will have been copied, iterated on, and potentially superseded by the time the patent office responds.

Design patents protect specific ornamental appearances -- [*this* specific animation](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1223&context=njtip) of a swipe gesture, not the concept of swiping. A competitor takes your concept, gives it a different visual treatment, and avoids infringement entirely.

Trade secrets require keeping the feature secret. But user-facing product features are, by definition, public. The moment a user interacts with your feature, it's visible to every competitor on the planet.

The most valuable things in product design -- the interaction patterns, the feature concepts, the design decisions, the product strategy that makes one tool feel effortless and another feel like homework -- exist in a legal no-man's-land. They are too abstract for patents, too functional for design patents, too idea-like for copyright, and too visible for trade secrets. They are, in every meaningful legal sense, unprotectable. And they are, in every meaningful business sense, the only things that matter.

The [Khan v. Figma](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2025-11-20-1814) class action, filed in November 2025, captured this dynamic with uncomfortable precision. The lawsuit alleged that Figma -- the design tool used by millions of product designers -- had secretly trained AI models on customer design files, including layer properties, text, images, and proprietary business IP. Figma's defense was revealing: ["Our training is focused on general patterns -- not on customers' unique content, concepts and ideas."](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2025-11-20-1814) This statement is almost certainly legally correct. General patterns aren't protected by copyright. Concepts and ideas aren't either. Figma was saying, in effect: we extracted the thing that has value -- your design patterns, your approaches, your system -- while leaving the thing that's legally protected, which is the specific pixel arrangement. The protection gap, stated plainly by the company exploiting it.

The question of AI inventorship added another layer. Dr. Stephen Thaler's [DABUS case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DABUS) -- in which he filed patent applications naming his AI system as the inventor -- was rejected by the [U.S. Federal Circuit](https://www.reedsmith.com/en/perspectives/2022/08/sorry-dabus-ai-cannot-be-an-inventor-on-a-us-patent), the [U.K. Supreme Court](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/uk/insights/articles-and-briefings/supreme-court-confirms-ai-cannot-be-inventor-under-patents-act), and the [European Patent Office](https://patentlyo.com/patent/2024/12/denied-european-patent.html). Only South Africa granted the patent. The consensus was clear: only natural persons can be named as inventors. The [USPTO's revised guidance from November 2025](https://www.uspto.gov/subscription-center/2025/revised-inventorship-guidance-ai-assisted-inventions) reinforced this, treating AI as merely a "tool that assists" in the inventive process, analogous to laboratory equipment. Human inventors must "conceive the invention with a definite and permanent idea."

The paradox is structural. The more AI contributes to the inventive process -- generating the concept, designing the implementation, writing the code -- the less the human operator can claim to have "conceived" the invention. The dominant pattern in AI-assisted development is precisely this: the human provides the intent and the AI provides the invention. Under current law, the result may not be patentable at all. The most productive use of AI in product development produces the least protectable output. The tool that makes invention easier makes protection harder.

## What Comes After

The pattern of history suggests adaptation. Every major reproduction technology -- the printing press, the player piano, the VCR, the internet -- triggered the same cycle. Incumbents declared it would destroy their industry. Courts struggled to apply old frameworks. The technology won some version of the fight. Legal frameworks adapted. Some creators were harmed, but culture was enriched. John Philip Sousa testified to Congress in 1906 that phonographs would ["ruin the artistic development of music in this country"](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/john-philip-sousa-feared-menace-mechanical-music-180967063/) and warned that children would "become simply human phonographs -- without soul or expression." Jack Valenti told Congress in 1982 that the VCR was ["to the American film producer and the American public as the Boston strangler is to the woman home alone."](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Valenti) The home video market became one of the most lucrative revenue streams in film history. The phonograph gave rise to the recording industry.

The optimistic reading of the AI moment follows this pattern. Yes, copying is easier. But the answer isn't legal protection -- it's the same set of non-legal advantages the tech industry has been developing for decades. Network effects. Proprietary data. Embedded workflows. Speed of iteration. Brand trust. Community. The things you can't copy by describing a feature to an AI assistant.

There's evidence for this view. Open source software -- which is, by design, freely copyable -- has produced some of the most successful technology platforms in history. Linux, React, Kubernetes, PostgreSQL. The value doesn't reside in the code, which anyone can copy. It resides in the ecosystem: the community of contributors, the documentation, the integrations, the institutional knowledge, the brand. If you can't protect the code, you build a world around the code that makes copying insufficient. The product becomes a platform. The feature becomes a network. The invention becomes an ecosystem. Figma itself is the proof: its value was never in any particular design tool feature (all of which could be replicated) but in the fact that every designer's team was already using it. The switching cost wasn't technical. It was social.

The less optimistic reading notes that each previous technology panic, while ultimately resolved, caused genuine harm along the way. Player pianos did destroy some musicians' livelihoods. Napster did devastate the music industry before streaming emerged. The resolution was real but so was the damage, and the damage was not evenly distributed. Major record labels survived the MP3 revolution. Independent musicians took the hit. The sewing machine patent pool worked for the founding companies. Smaller manufacturers were less fortunate. There is a pattern within the pattern: the powerful adapt, and the adaptation often comes at the expense of the less powerful.

The music industry's response to AI shows both sides. Major labels -- UMG, Sony, Warner -- [sued AI music generators Suno and Udio](https://www.riaa.com/record-companies-bring-landmark-cases-for-responsible-ai-againstsuno-and-udio-in-boston-and-new-york-federal-courts-respectively/) in June 2024. Then they settled and signed licensing deals. Warner partnered with Suno on a licensed AI music platform. Universal settled with Udio. The litigation became a negotiation became a business relationship. But independent musicians, who filed separate class actions in October 2025, got no such deal. Licensing works for entities that have something to license at scale. It doesn't work for a solo designer whose interaction pattern was absorbed into a training dataset and reproduced, without attribution, a thousand times.

This asymmetry maps precisely onto the Sewing Machine Combination of 1856 and the Manufacturer's Aircraft Association of 1917. In both cases, the major players formed pools and shared the spoils. In both cases, smaller players were either licensed on unfavorable terms or excluded entirely. The resolution preserved the industry but not the inventor. The grand bargain, even when it technically held, distributed its benefits unevenly. The system has always worked better for those with the resources to work the system.

The question the historical pattern doesn't answer is whether this disruption is qualitatively different. In every previous case, the reproduction technology copied *artifacts* -- books, recordings, broadcasts, software binaries. The originals retained a form of scarcity because creating new ones still required human creative effort. AI doesn't just copy artifacts. It absorbs the *capacity* for creation. It learns the patterns, the approaches, the style, the logic -- the things that have value precisely because they represent accumulated skill and judgment -- and reproduces them on demand. The copying isn't of the work. It's of the worker.

The grand bargain was designed for a world where the main threat was someone building a copy of your cotton gin. It was not designed for a world where someone can build a machine that understands the *principle* of your cotton gin and generates novel variations faster than you can file a patent application.

And there is a second structural difference. The grand bargain requires disclosure as the price of protection -- you reveal how your invention works, and in exchange, you get a monopoly. But the bargain assumes the monopoly is valuable enough to justify the disclosure. When AI collapses the time between disclosure and replication to near-zero, the price doesn't change but the compensation does. You still have to tell the world how your invention works. You just don't get a meaningful window of exclusivity in return. The bargain becomes asymmetric: all cost, no benefit. Rational actors, recognizing this, will choose not to disclose -- will choose trade secrets over patents, opacity over transparency. The grand bargain doesn't just break for inventors. It breaks for society. The public storehouse of knowledge that patent disclosures were supposed to create goes empty.

Some see a third option: abandoning the concept of protection entirely and competing on execution alone. Build faster than anyone can copy. Ship before competitors know you've started. Iterate while they're still understanding your first version. This is, in practice, what most software startups already do. The shift from "protect, then build" to "build, then build faster" predates AI by a decade. AI simply makes the alternative -- protection through legal mechanisms -- feel even more futile.

But speed has a ceiling. Not every product can be built by a small, fast team. Not every innovation is a single feature that can be iterated weekly. Some of the most important product work -- design systems, accessibility infrastructure, editorial standards, institutional knowledge embedded in workflows -- accumulates slowly, across years, by teams that can't be compressed. Speed-as-moat advantages the trivial over the substantial, the incremental over the foundational. It's a strategy that works until you need to build something that takes patience.

Whether this difference is one of degree or kind is the question the system hasn't answered yet. Jefferson, who worried about ideas that "force themselves into the possession of every one," might have recognized the current moment with something between alarm and resigned amusement. His candle metaphor -- he who lights his taper at mine receives light without darkening me -- assumed the tapers were lit one at a time, by individual hands. He didn't account for a machine that could light ten million tapers in a second. The flame is the same. The velocity is not.

The grand bargain rests on the assumption that disclosure is tolerable because the monopoly window provides adequate compensation. When the window shrinks to zero -- when disclosure *is* replication -- the bargain isn't unfair. It simply ceases to exist. What takes its place, for the designers and engineers and product thinkers building software in 2026, is not a legal framework but a competitive one: build faster than anyone can copy, embed deeper than anyone can extract, and create value that can't be described in a prompt.

Whether that's enough is an open question. Whitney couldn't protect the cotton gin, and the cotton gin changed the world anyway -- just not in a way that benefited Whitney. Bell's telephone patent, by contrast, made him rich beyond imagination -- but it also created a monopoly that arguably slowed telecommunications innovation for a century. The Wright brothers' patent ruined American aviation. Edison's patent empire couldn't save him from a better technology. The history of patents is not a story about protection working or failing. It's a story about the unpredictable distance between having a right and being able to use it, between the law on paper and the world as it actually operates.

The grand bargain broke once before, at the very beginning, for the very first invention that mattered. The system adapted. But it took a long time, and the inventor went bankrupt.

Brunelleschi's boat sank. He got the patent anyway.
