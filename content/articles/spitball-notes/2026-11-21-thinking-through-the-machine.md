# Spitball: Thinking Through the Machine

**Date:** 2026-11-21
**Status:** Raw notes, dictated stream of consciousness plus the back-and-forth. Not an outline.

---

## Threads

**1. Concentration and homogenization of thought.** AI expands thinking, but it also funnels all of humanity's thinking through one interface owned by profit-driven companies. Even a "pure" tool with no profit motive would standardize thought: same defaults, same framings, same "reasonable" answers. Open tension: how is this different from everyone reading the same encyclopedia or using Google? Possible answer: AI doesn't just supply information, it does the composing.

**2. Can technology be neutral?** Every tool seems to carry the worldview and needs of the culture that made it.

**3. Cognitive colonization.** Mass tech is mostly American-made, and it spreads American assumptions (individualism, market logic, efficiency, ways of talking) more deeply than military power, because it works on how people think. Counterpoint to test: local appropriation (WhatsApp culture in Spain, Latin American meme cultures). Colonized or absorbed? Probably both, unevenly. LLMs trained mostly on English make this sharper.

**4. Dictation and the perfect scribe.** Writing splits into speaking first, editing later. Not entirely new: Milton dictated *Paradise Lost*, Dostoevsky dictated to his stenographer, and Henry James's late style loosened once he dictated. What's new is that everyone has the scribe. Question: what did the friction of writing do for thought? Does voice-first writing pull us back toward oral habits (repetition, digression) while still keeping a record?

**5. Smarter or dumber?** Personal evidence: worse spelling, attention and reading. It can be measured, and studies are arriving:
- CMU / Oxford / MIT / UCLA study (2026): AI improves immediate performance "at a heavy cognitive cost." When access was cut, scores and persistence dropped. People who asked for direct solutions declined the most.
- Microsoft-linked survey of knowledge workers: more trust in AI meant less critical thinking.
- Gerlich (2025, *Societies*): 666 participants, negative correlation between AI use and critical thinking, strongest in younger users.
- Caveat: attention and reading were eroding before AI (smartphones, feeds).
- Meta-observation: AI produces plausible-sounding but empty insight (e.g. a "thought monoculture" analogy that jumped without explanation). Convenience makes it easy to absorb the shape of an idea without the substance.

**6. Regulation and independent study.** Pharma, food, cars and weapons are regulated and studied independently; AI mostly isn't. Nuance: in the EU, the AI Office can enforce the AI Act's general-purpose AI obligations since Aug 2, 2026, but those cover documentation, transparency, copyright and safety, not long-term cognitive effects. Company programs for academics (e.g. OpenAI's free access for 100,000 researchers) give access to tools, not independent oversight with company data.

**7. Thinking without judgment.** Dictating to a machine removes the self-consciousness of talking to a human. Freeing, like journaling or confession. But ideas also get tested socially, through resistance, misunderstanding and boredom. Without that, thought could become more individualistic: a diary that talks back, with a sycophant on the other end.

**8. Business model: replacement vs. augmentation.** Subscriptions that augment workers probably won't justify the investment; paying to replace work will. Pushback from the session: sycophancy and labor replacement are separate arguments. Sycophancy comes largely from training on human approval ratings (people rate agreeable answers higher). The labor argument is the stronger one. Counterargument: automation has historically shifted jobs more than erased them. Nobody knows yet.

**9. Paying for intelligence.** Tiered access to higher intelligence based on money. Is "fascist" the right word? Probably not (fascism = authoritarian nationalism, crushing opposition). Closer: stratification, or Varoufakis's "technofeudalism." Money always bought cognitive advantage, but this version is rented and revocable, explicitly tiered by one company, and compounds (better tools mean more income mean better tools). Counter: free tiers keep improving, open-weight models exist, costs fall fast. Open question: does the frontier pull away faster than the floor rises? Irony: a smarter model may make offloading worse, because it's easier to trust.

## Reading List

- **Heidegger, *The Question Concerning Technology*** — technology as a way of revealing the world; "enframing" everything as a resource. Core text for the neutrality question.
- **Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?"** — the classic argument that technologies embody political choices.
- **Marshall McLuhan, *Understanding Media*** — "the medium is the message."
- **Ivan Illich, *Tools for Conviviality*** — tools that serve people vs. tools people end up serving.
- **Michel Foucault** — how techniques and practices shape subjects (connects to the Foucault translation work).
- **Walter Ong, *Orality and Literacy*** — writing restructured consciousness; "secondary orality" for electronic media. Key for the dictation thread.
- **Herbert Schiller, *Mass Communications and American Empire*** — cultural imperialism through media.
- **Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Decolonising the Mind*** — language as the vehicle of colonization; relevant to English-dominant LLMs.
- **Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*** — business models built on extracting human behavior.
- **Yanis Varoufakis, *Technofeudalism*** — renting access from platform owners.
- **Michael Gerlich, "AI Tools in Society" (*Societies*, 2025)** — cognitive offloading and critical thinking.
- **CMU / Oxford / MIT / UCLA study on AI assistance and persistence (2026)** — measurable short-term cognitive costs.
