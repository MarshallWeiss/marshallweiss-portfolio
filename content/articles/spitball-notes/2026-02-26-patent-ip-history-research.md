## I. Origins of Patent Law

### The Philosophical Foundation: The "Grand Bargain"

The patent system rests on a **quid pro quo**: in exchange for the complete public disclosure of a new and useful invention, the government grants the inventor a temporary, legally enforceable monopoly -- typically for 20 years from filing. The inventor gets exclusivity; society gets knowledge.

The notion of the "patent bargain" was introduced in 1792 by Joseph Barnes, one of the early American patent commentators. Under the bargain, "immediate disclosure is not the objective of, but is exacted from, the patentee. It is the price paid for the exclusivity secured." ([Texas A&M Law Scholarship](https://scholarship.law.tamu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1806&context=facscholar))

Patent disclosures form what scholars call an "invisible college of technology" -- a public storehouse of knowledge that benefits all of society. The patent specification must describe the invention and the process of making and using it "sufficient to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use the invention." ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=7a84b640-fb22-4c61-b5fe-b392b6d31e80))

**Why this matters for the article:** The grand bargain assumes that disclosure itself has value -- that knowing how something works is useful to society even if you can't make it for 20 years. In the AI era, this assumption is tested: disclosure + AI means anyone can replicate the disclosed invention almost immediately. The "20-year head start" collapses.

### How Patent Law Differs from Copyright (From the Start)

| | Patent | Copyright |
|--|--------|-----------|
| **Protects** | Inventions, processes, methods, machines | Expression (text, images, code as written) |
| **Does NOT protect** | Abstract ideas, laws of nature, natural phenomena | Ideas, facts, methods, systems |
| **Requires** | Novelty, non-obviousness, utility, disclosure | Originality, fixation in tangible medium |
| **Registration** | Mandatory (must apply to patent office) | Automatic (exists upon creation) |
| **Term** | 20 years from filing | Life + 70 years (or 95 for corporate) |
| **Cost** | $15,000-$30,000+ for utility patent | $35-$85 for registration |
| **Time to obtain** | 3-5 years typical | Near-instant |
| **Enforcement** | Must actively litigate | Must actively litigate |

The fundamental distinction: **copyright protects how you express an idea; patents protect the idea itself** (as embodied in an invention). This is why the article's thesis pivots from copyright to patents -- if the question is "how do you protect a product idea," copyright is categorically the wrong tool.

### Brunelleschi's Patent (1421) -- The First Known Patent

In **1421**, the architect Filippo Brunelleschi -- the man who designed the dome of the Florence Cathedral, one of the greatest engineering achievements in history -- received what is thought to be the first modern patent in the Western world. ([ETB Law](https://www.etblaw.com/what-was-the-first-patent/), [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/patent/1159928/the-first-patent-now-celebrating-600-years-of-age))

The patent was for a river transport vessel -- a barge called *Il Badalone* -- designed to carry marble from Pisa to Florence up the River Arno "for less money than usual, and with several other benefits."

**The anecdote:** Brunelleschi was paranoid about competitors -- particularly his former partner Lorenzo Ghiberti -- stealing the design. Despite the patent, he remained extremely secretive, sharing only crude details with the engineer Mariano Taccola. In **1427**, Brunelleschi finally built Il Badalone. **It sank on its maiden voyage**, along with a sizable portion of his personal fortune. ([KnowFuture](https://knowfuture.wordpress.com/2011/03/09/il-badalone-brunnelleschi-and-the-first-patent/))

**Why this matters:** The very first patent holder was simultaneously paranoid about theft and unable to make his invention work. The tension between protection and practical execution is as old as patents themselves.

### The Venetian Patent Statute (1474) -- The First Patent System

On **March 19, 1474**, the Senate of the Republic of Venice enacted the world's first codified patent system. The statute provided that patents could be granted for "any new and ingenious device, not previously made," provided it was useful. The reward: **ten years** of exclusive protection. Anyone who made a similar device without the inventor's consent could be fined 100 ducats, and the infringing device destroyed. ([Wikipedia: Venetian Patent Statute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Patent_Statute), [Copyright History Project](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_i_1474))

**Key features of the statute:**
- Required novelty (the device must be "not previously made")
- Required utility (it must work)
- Required registration
- Granted 10-year protection
- Provided for infringement penalties

Between 1474 and 1788, the Venetian Senate granted about 2,000 patents: 28 between 1474 and 1500, 593 between 1500 and 1600. The system was remarkably sophisticated for its era.

**Academic debate:** Some historians argue the statute was merely "a codification of prior customs" rather than a true innovation. The majority view is that it marked a watershed: the first time a state systematized the granting of exclusive rights for inventions. ([Northwestern JTIP](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1334&context=njtip))

### The English Statute of Monopolies (1624) -- Patents Split from Royal Patronage

**Background:** In England, "letters patent" had long been used by monarchs to grant monopolies over industries -- not to reward innovation, but to raise revenue and reward allies. Elizabeth I became notorious for granting patents on common commodities like starch and salt, causing public outrage. Her successor, James I, used the system even more aggressively. ([Wikipedia: Statute of Monopolies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Monopolies))

The **Statute of Monopolies (1624)** was Parliament's response. It abolished all existing monopolies with one critical exception: **patents for novel inventions**. The statute established that grants for "new manufacture[s]" could be made to "the true and first inventor" for a term of 14 years. ([Copyright History](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_uk_1624))

The statute has been called "one of the landmarks in the transition of [England's] economy from the feudal to the capitalist." It remained the basis for UK patent law until 1977 and is still the foundation of Australian patent law today. ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=6436cf59-1a6b-4b02-8dde-484255b39a2e))

**Why this matters:** The Statute of Monopolies established the principle that patents exist to incentivize invention, not to reward political allies. The 14-year term was based on the idea of two full training cycles for apprentices -- enough time for the inventor to train two generations of workers in the new technique before the monopoly expired. The public benefit rationale was there from the beginning.

### Thomas Jefferson and the American Patent System (1790)

The U.S. Constitution explicitly authorized patents: Congress may grant exclusive rights "to promote the progress of science and useful arts" (Article I, Section 8, Clause 8). Note the purpose: **promote progress**, not enrich inventors.

The **Patent Act of 1790** created the first American patent system. The first patent board consisted of Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, Secretary of War Henry Knox, and Attorney General Edmund Randolph. Jefferson was, in effect, **America's first patent examiner**. ([Monticello: Patents](https://www.monticello.org/research-education/thomas-jefferson-encyclopedia/patents/), [USPTO Milestones](https://www.uspto.gov/patents/milestones))

**Jefferson's approach was rigorous.** During his tenure (1790-1793), only 57 patents were approved out of 114 applications. He often insisted on testing the invention himself, not just reading specifications. He was deeply skeptical of monopolies and concerned about their potential to restrict the commons.

Jefferson's 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson contains the most quoted passage in IP philosophy:

> "If nature has made any one thing less susceptible than all others of exclusive property, it is the action of the thinking power called an idea, which an individual may exclusively possess as long as he keeps it to himself; but the moment it is divulged, it forces itself into the possession of every one, and the receiver cannot dispossess himself of it."

And the candle metaphor:

> "He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me."

Jefferson acknowledged patents as potentially useful policy but insisted they were a **pragmatic concession**, not a natural right. ([University of Chicago Press](https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/a1_8_8s12.html))

**Why this matters:** Jefferson's skepticism anticipated every modern debate about patents. He understood that ideas resist exclusivity -- that the patent system works *against* the natural tendency of knowledge to spread. In the AI era, where knowledge spreads at machine speed, his concerns are more relevant than ever.

## II. Industrial Revolution Patent Wars

### Eli Whitney and the Cotton Gin (1794) -- When Patents Fail

Eli Whitney received his patent for the cotton gin in 1794. It was arguably the most transformative American invention of the early industrial age -- it made cotton farming massively profitable and, catastrophically, entrenched slavery in the American South. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/cotton-gin-patent))

**But Whitney made almost no money from it.**

The device was so mechanically simple that anyone could copy it. Due to a loophole in the 1793 patent law, Whitney couldn't collect damages from copycats until the law was amended in 1800. Even then, lawsuits consumed his profits. His cotton gin company went bankrupt in 1797. His patent wasn't fully validated until 1807 -- and Congress refused to renew it when it expired that same year. ([Wikipedia: Eli Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Whitney), [Bill of Rights Institute](https://billofrightsinstitute.org/essays/eli-whitney-and-the-cotton-gin))

Whitney's bitter conclusion: **"An invention can be so valuable as to be worthless to the inventor."**

**Why this matters:** Whitney's story is the original version of today's software copying problem. A simple, easily replicable invention -- no matter how transformative -- can be nearly impossible to protect. The patent existed; enforcement was the problem. This is exactly the dynamic AI accelerates.

### The Sewing Machine Wars (1850s) -- The First Patent Thicket

The sewing machine was the smartphone of its era: a complex product requiring multiple patented technologies, no single inventor, and overlapping claims that produced legal gridlock.

**The problem:** The functional sewing machine required several key innovations -- the lockstitch (Elias Howe), the four-motion feed, the vertical needle with horizontal sewing surface, and others. Each was patented by a different inventor. Every sewing machine produced, sold, or used infringed multiple patents, spawning "massive litigation by the patent-owners against manufacturers, retailers, and consumers alike." ([Arizona Law Review](https://arizonalawreview.org/mossoff/), [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1354849))

**The solution:** In 1856, Orlando B. Potter (president of Grover & Baker) brokered what became the **Sewing Machine Combination** -- the first privately formed patent pool in American history. Howe, Wheeler & Wilson, Singer, and Grover & Baker pooled nine key patents and agreed to cross-license them. At least 24 manufacturers were licensed; founding companies shared profits equally; Howe received a $5 royalty per machine. The pool lasted until the last patent expired in 1877. ([Wikipedia: Sewing Machine Combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewing_Machine_Combination), [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2013/12/sewing-machine-patent-wars-of-the-1850s-what-they-tell-us-about-the-patent-system.html))

**Why this matters:** The sewing machine wars are the direct ancestor of modern smartphone patent thickets. The pattern is identical: multiple overlapping patents on components of a complex product, massive litigation, eventual resolution through pooling/cross-licensing. Adam Mossoff (law professor who studied this extensively) argues this shows that patent thickets are real but solvable -- the market finds solutions through private ordering. Critics argue it shows the system is inherently broken for complex technologies.

### Alexander Graham Bell and the Telephone (1876) -- The Most Valuable Patent in History

On **February 14, 1876**, attorneys for both Alexander Graham Bell and Elisha Gray submitted telephone-related documents to the U.S. Patent Office on the same day. The Patent Office ruled that Bell's application arrived first. Bell received his patent on March 7, 1876 -- what has been called "the most valuable single patent ever issued." ([Wikipedia: Bell-Gray Controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Gray_and_Alexander_Bell_telephone_controversy))

**The controversy:** Several conspiracy theories emerged during trials and appeals (1878-1888). Patent examiner Zenas Wilber was accused of revealing secret information from Gray's application to Bell and his attorneys. Some historians have claimed a clerk with a drinking problem was bribed to alter filing records.

More recent scholarship has challenged these allegations. A 2020 paper by Professor Benjamin Brown of Marquette University concluded that Bell did conceive of the working telephone before Gray. ([Marquette University](https://www.marquette.edu/news-center/2020/marquette-professor-settles-144-year-controversy-on-invention-of-the-telephone.php))

**The legal aftermath:** The Bell Telephone Company faced over 600 patent challenges. In **The Telephone Cases (1888)**, the Supreme Court upheld Bell's patents. The Bell patent monopoly shaped the American telecommunications industry for the next century. ([Wikipedia: The Telephone Cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Telephone_Cases))

**Why this matters:** The Bell patent is the ultimate example of a patent creating a massive monopoly from a single filing. It also shows how patents can turn on hours -- or minutes -- of timing. The race between Bell and Gray, and the allegations of corruption, illustrate that the patent system has always been subject to gaming.

### Edison's Patent Empire (1869-1931)

Thomas Edison held **1,093 U.S. patents** and an additional 1,239 foreign patents, totaling 2,332 worldwide -- making him the most prolific patent holder in American history. ([Thomas Edison.org](https://www.thomasedison.org/edison-patents))

Edison's approach was strategic, not just inventive:
- He meticulously recorded his work in **4,000 notebooks** to protect his intellectual property
- In 1882, at the height of his work on electric light and power, he completed **106 successful applications** in a single year
- His inventions weren't isolated devices -- they were **systems of interconnected technologies** (electric lighting networks, phonograph recording systems, motion picture cameras and projectors)

**The War of Currents:** Edison's most famous patent war was against George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla over AC vs. DC electricity. Edison's campaign went far beyond legal action -- he staged public demonstrations where animals were electrocuted using Westinghouse's AC current, including (infamously) an elephant. He lobbied to have the electric chair powered by AC, specifically to associate his competitor's technology with death. ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/edison-vs-westinghouse-a-shocking-rivalry-102146036/))

**The outcome:** Edison lost. In 1892, financier J.P. Morgan forced Edison General Electric to switch to AC power and then pushed Edison out of the company he had founded. Edison later admitted he regretted not taking Tesla's advice. ([EDN](https://www.edn.com/war-of-currents-tesla-vs-edison/))

**Why this matters:** Edison demonstrates that patents alone don't win wars -- and that the patent holder isn't always right. His AC/DC campaign was a combination of patent strategy, propaganda, and political manipulation. The War of Currents shows that when fundamental technological choices are at stake, patents become weapons in a much larger battle.

### The Wright Brothers -- Pioneers of Flight and Patent Trolling

The Wright brothers received their patent for a method of airplane flight control in **1906**. In 1909, they sold it to the newly formed Wright Company for $100,000 in cash, 40% stock, and a 10% royalty on all aircraft sold. ([Wikipedia: Wright brothers patent war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers_patent_war))

**The litigation campaign:** The Wright Company waged aggressive patent warfare, initially trying to secure a monopoly on all U.S. aircraft manufacturing. When that failed, they sued domestic and foreign aviators and companies, filing **12 major lawsuits** -- most notably against Glenn Curtiss, who used ailerons (small flaps on wing tips) instead of the Wrights' wing-warping system. The Wrights argued that ailerons fell within their patent's scope. ([TIME: Wright Brothers Patent Trolling](https://time.com/4143574/wright-brothers-patent-trolling/))

**The catastrophic result:** During the period of intense litigation, the Wrights "sorely neglected R&D -- so much so that by 1915, when Orville sold the company, their aircraft were derided as dangerous and obsolete." By the time the U.S. entered World War I in 1917, **the government could not find a single American aircraft fit for military service**. The state of domestic aviation was so dismal that the newly established National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics pressured the industry to form the **Manufacturer's Aircraft Association** -- a cross-licensing patent pool modeled on the Sewing Machine Combination. ([Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/flying-machines/04-patent-wars/), [Simple Flying](https://simpleflying.com/general-aviation-wright-brothers-patent-war/))

TIME has called the Wrights "pioneers of what's sometimes known as patent trolling: the controversial modern practice of suing competitors for infringements that fall beyond the scope of one's patent."

**Why this matters:** The Wright brothers story is the definitive example of patents *hindering* rather than *helping* innovation. The brothers who invented powered flight used their patent to prevent the development of better aircraft -- and the result was that America, the birthplace of aviation, fell behind Europe in aircraft technology. This is the nightmare scenario that patent critics invoke: the legal monopoly becomes more valuable than the actual innovation, and the system rewards litigation over invention.

## III. Software Patents: The Contested Frontier

### The Early Trilogy: Can You Patent Software?

Three Supreme Court decisions established the initial framework:

**Gottschalk v. Benson (1972):** The Court held that a patent on a method for converting binary-coded decimal numbers to pure binary numbers would "wholly pre-empt the mathematical formula." Mathematical algorithms, standing alone, cannot be patented. ([Wikipedia: Gottschalk v. Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottschalk_v._Benson))

**Parker v. Flook (1978):** The Court ruled that Flook's method for updating alarm limits was unpatentable -- not because it contained a mathematical algorithm, but because "once that algorithm is assumed to be within the prior art, the application, considered as a whole, contains no patentable invention." The mere application of a known formula to a known process isn't enough. ([Wikipedia: Parker v. Flook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_v._Flook))

**Diamond v. Diehr (1981):** In a crucial **5-4 decision**, the Court held that a process for curing synthetic rubber -- which used a computer program to calculate cure times using a mathematical equation -- WAS patentable. "The mere presence of a software element did not make an otherwise patent-eligible machine or process patent ineligible." The key: the invention, *considered as a whole*, was a physical process (transforming rubber), not a mathematical abstraction. ([Wikipedia: Diamond v. Diehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_v._Diehr))

**The takeaway from the trilogy:** Pure algorithms and mathematical formulas can't be patented. But a process that *uses* software as part of a larger, concrete invention? That can. The line between the two was -- and remains -- deeply unclear.

### State Street Bank (1998) -- The Floodgates Open

**State Street Bank & Trust Co. v. Signature Financial Group (1998)** was the Federal Circuit decision that changed everything. The court ruled that a data processing system for a "Hub and Spoke" mutual fund investment structure was patentable. The key holding: **business methods are no longer an exception to patentable subject matter**, as long as they produce a "useful, concrete and tangible result." ([Wikipedia: State Street Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Street_Bank_%26_Trust_Co._v._Signature_Financial_Group,_Inc.))

**The explosion:** State Street opened the gates for both software and business method patents on a massive scale. In 1991, software-related patents represented about 20% of yearly totals; by 2011, they accounted for about 50%. The absolute number rose from ~25,000 per year to ~125,000 per year. ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=8cdd3dd7-1fb3-48dc-a7ba-b6ffb0076e8e))

### Amazon's One-Click Patent (1999) -- The Poster Child for Bad Patents

In September 1999, the USPTO granted Amazon a patent on "one-click" ordering -- the ability to purchase an item with a single click by storing payment and shipping information. Amazon immediately sued Barnes & Noble, which offered a similar "Express Lane" feature. A district court issued an injunction forcing Barnes & Noble to stop -- during the 1999 holiday shopping season. ([Wikipedia: 1-Click](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-Click), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2017/09/06/548819270/amazon-s-1-click-patent-is-about-to-expire-what-s-the-big-deal))

The Federal Circuit vacated the injunction in 2001, questioning whether one-click was truly novel compared to earlier "shopping cart" systems. But the patent survived until it expired in 2017.

**Why this matters:** The one-click patent became the poster child for the absurdity of software/business method patents. Critics argued it gave Amazon "an unfair monopoly on what amounts to little more than an efficient means of using standard technology" -- and that other websites had to make their shopping systems "deliberately less efficient to avoid infringement." The patent existed not to protect a genuine invention, but to tax a basic interaction pattern.

### Bilski v. Kappos (2010) -- The Supreme Court Steps In

The Supreme Court unanimously rejected Bilski's patent on a method of hedging risk in energy markets, calling it an unpatentable "abstract idea." But the Court declined to create a bright-line rule excluding business methods or software from patentability. The machine-or-transformation test was "a useful and important clue" but not the only test. ([Wikipedia: Bilski v. Kappos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilski_v._Kappos))

Justice Stevens' concurrence (joined by three others) argued for a categorical exclusion of business method patents, but this wasn't the majority position. The case left the fundamental question -- *when is software patentable?* -- largely unresolved.

### Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank (2014) -- The Earthquake

**The most consequential patent case of the 21st century.**

Alice Corporation held patents on a computer-implemented scheme for mitigating financial "settlement risk" using a third-party intermediary. In a **unanimous decision**, the Supreme Court held that an abstract idea does not become patent-eligible simply by being implemented on a generic computer. ([Wikipedia: Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Corp._v._CLS_Bank_International), [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/573/208/))

The Court established a two-step test (building on *Mayo v. Prometheus*, 2012):
1. **Step 1:** Is the claim directed to a patent-ineligible concept (abstract idea, law of nature, natural phenomenon)?
2. **Step 2:** If yes, does the claim contain an "inventive concept" -- something significantly more than the abstract idea itself?

**The devastation:**
- Patent issuance for business methods dropped by **more than half** in the months following Alice
- Of 76 Federal Circuit decisions dealing with Alice challenges through 2015, **57 invalidated patents** (75%) and only 16 upheld them
- An estimated **82.9% invalidation rate** for computer-implemented patents if challenged
- Of ~240,000 U.S. patents related to computer-implemented inventions in 2015, an estimated **199,000 would be found invalid** if challenged under Alice
- Section 101 rejections jumped from 7% to 15% of all denied applications by August 2015

([EFF: Saved by Alice](https://www.eff.org/alice), [Berkeley Technology Law Journal](https://btlj.org/2016/03/alice-abstract-ideas-software-related-patents/), [USPTO: Adjusting to Alice](https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/OCE-DH_AdjustingtoAlice.pdf))

**Why this matters for the article:** Alice made it dramatically harder to patent software methods. Combined with AI making software features trivially replicable, this creates the **perfect storm** for product designers: the legal mechanism that *could* protect a product idea (utility patents) has been significantly weakened at exactly the moment when the threat of copying has become most severe.

### The Patent Troll Phenomenon

**The NTP v. BlackBerry case** is the canonical example. NTP, Inc. -- a Virginia-based patent holding company founded in 1992 with 50 U.S. patents -- sued Research in Motion (maker of BlackBerry) for patent infringement in 2001. NTP never manufactured anything. The case settled with RIM paying NTP **$612.5 million** -- far greater than the value of the infringing technology. ([Wikipedia: NTP, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTP,_Inc.), [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-story-behind-the-blackberry-case))

**Scale of the problem:**
- In 2011, U.S. businesses incurred **$29 billion in direct costs** from patent trolls
- From 2009 through mid-2013, Apple was the defendant in **171 NPE lawsuits**, followed by HP (137), Samsung (133), AT&T (127), and Dell (122)
- Patent trolls (non-practicing entities/NPEs) acquire patents not to produce goods or services but solely to extract licensing fees or lawsuit settlements

([Wikipedia: Patent troll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent_troll))

**Why this matters:** The patent troll problem demonstrates that the patent system can be weaponized for rent-seeking rather than innovation. It also explains why many in Silicon Valley are hostile to patents -- they've experienced them primarily as weapons used against them, not tools protecting their own innovations.

## IV. Design Patents and Trade Dress

### Design Patents vs. Utility Patents

| | Design Patent | Utility Patent |
|--|---------------|----------------|
| **Protects** | Ornamental appearance ("how it looks") | Function, structure, composition ("how it works") |
| **Term** | 15 years from grant | 20 years from filing |
| **Cost** | $2,800-$4,500 | $15,000-$30,000+ |
| **Time to obtain** | 1-2 years | 3-5 years |
| **Maintenance fees** | None | Required |
| **Standard** | Novel, non-obvious ornamental design | Novel, non-obvious, useful |

### Apple v. Samsung (2012-2018) -- Design Patents Go Nuclear

The Apple v. Samsung case was the most significant design patent dispute in modern history. Apple sued Samsung for infringing three design patents:
- **D618,677**: A black rectangular front face with rounded corners
- **D593,087**: A rectangular front face with rounded corners and a raised rim
- **D604,305**: A grid of 16 colorful icons on a black screen

A jury found Samsung infringed all three and awarded Apple **$399 million** -- Samsung's **entire profit** from the sale of its infringing smartphones. ([Justia: Samsung v. Apple](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/580/15-777/))

**The Supreme Court weighed in (2016):** In a unanimous decision, the Court reversed the damages calculation. For a multicomponent product, the relevant "article of manufacture" for damages "need not be the end product sold to the consumer but may be only a component." A phone is more than its screen shape. But the Court didn't resolve *which* component was relevant -- that went back to the lower courts. ([Supreme Court Opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/16pdf/15-777_7lho.pdf))

**The final settlement (2018):** Samsung paid Apple **$533 million** after a retrial. ([Wolf Greenfield](https://wolfgreenfield.com/ip-alerts/533-million-apple-v-samsung-verdict-highlights-importance-of-design-patents))

**Why this matters:** Apple v. Samsung proved that design patents have real teeth -- a half-billion-dollar verdict for "rounded rectangles." But it also showed the absurdity: should the *shape* of a phone -- a rectangular slab with rounded corners, which is essentially the only practical shape for a touchscreen device -- be protectable? The case fueled the debate about whether design patents protect genuine innovation or merely tax the obvious.

### UI/UX Design Patents -- What You Can (and Can't) Protect

Design patents can protect specific GUI elements:

- **Apple's "Slide to Unlock" (D621,849):** Apple patented the visual metaphor of sliding a bolt to unlock a phone. Samsung paid **$120 million** for using a similar pattern. ([IP Business Academy](https://ipbusinessacademy.org/animated-user-interface-design-patents-in-the-us-a-case-study-of-the-slide-to-unlock-design-by-apple))
- **Tinder's Swipe Gesture:** Tinder has owned the patent on the swipe-left/swipe-right interaction layout since 2017. ([NPR: Tinder-Bumble Feud](https://www.npr.org/2018/10/30/660006488/the-tinder-bumble-feud-dating-apps-fight-over-who-owns-the-swipe))
- **Facebook's Animated Like Button:** Facebook holds exclusive rights to animated thumbs-up/like icons in UI since 2016 in the US, Canada, and Korea.
- **Apple's iMessage Chat Bubbles:** Apple patented the chat bubble tail design in 2013 (granted 2015).

**The limitations:**
- A GUI design must be **non-functional** to qualify for design patent protection (functional aspects need utility patents)
- The design must be **novel and non-obvious**
- Design patents protect only the **specific ornamental appearance** -- not the *concept* of swiping, unlocking, or liking
- A competitor can create a functionally identical feature with a visually distinct implementation and avoid infringement
- Design patents are narrow: they protect *this specific swipe animation*, not *the idea of swiping to make a choice*

([Northwestern JTIP: "Ooey GUI"](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1223&context=njtip), [PatentPC](https://patentpc.com/blog/can-software-design-be-patented))

### Trade Dress Protection for Software

Trade dress protects the overall commercial image or "look and feel" of a product. For software:
- The GUI's appearance can qualify as trade dress if it is **nonfunctional** and either **inherently distinctive** or has **acquired secondary meaning** (consumers associate the look with a specific source)
- Trade dress claims for software UI are rare and difficult to win
- Apple's approach in the Samsung case included trade dress claims alongside design patents and utility patents -- a comprehensive strategy

**The problem:** Trade dress for software UI is extremely narrow. It protects against someone making a product that looks confusingly similar to yours. It does NOT protect against someone taking your *idea* and implementing it with a different visual treatment. If your competitor copies your feature but makes it look different, trade dress doesn't help. ([IPO White Paper](https://ipo.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/PracticalApproachtoProtectionofGUIinUS.pdf))

## V. The Tech Industry and IP: How Companies Actually Compete

### Speed as the Primary Moat (Not Patents)

The tech industry's actual competitive advantages have little to do with patents:

**Network effects:** Once a platform reaches critical mass, the value it provides to each user increases with every additional user. Facebook, LinkedIn, Uber -- network effects create moats that patents cannot. ([NFX: Network Effects Manual](https://www.nfx.com/post/network-effects-manual))

**Data advantages:** Proprietary data -- from user behavior, from specialized collection, from embedded workflows -- creates advantages competitors can't replicate by copying features alone. Though Andreessen Horowitz has argued that **"data moats are rarely durable"** because data advantages erode as competitors collect similar information. ([a16z: The Empty Promise of Data Moats](https://a16z.com/the-empty-promise-of-data-moats/))

**Speed of iteration:** "Technological advantages have a short half-life and aren't very defensible anymore -- if you're first to market, competition will copy your technology or develop it themselves before long." Speed is one of the few advantages a startup has against an incumbent. ([Greylock: The New New Moats](https://greylock.com/greymatter/the-new-new-moats/))

**Embedded workflows:** Products that become integral to users' workflows (Slack, Figma, Notion) create switching costs that patents can't replicate.

**Brand and trust:** In the AI era, "data moats are no longer about ownership; they're about learning velocity -- the faster a product improves itself through user feedback, the harder it becomes to displace."

### The Fast-Follower Problem (Pre-AI)

Copying was already the norm before AI made it trivial:

**Facebook vs. Snapchat:** In August 2016, Facebook launched Instagram Stories -- a near-exact copy of Snapchat Stories. In Snapchat's 2017 IPO filing, they acknowledged that Instagram Stories "largely mimics our Stories feature." In the months after launch, **Snapchat's growth slowed 82%**. Facebook eventually added Snapchat-style Stories to all four of its core apps: Facebook, Messenger, WhatsApp, and Instagram. ([CNBC: Facebook Copies Snapchat](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/03/09/facebook-copies-snapchat-examples.html))

Snapchat CEO Evan Spiegel mocked Mark Zuckerberg on LinkedIn in February 2025, calling himself "the VP of Product at Meta" because of how many features they'd replicated. ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/02/06/snap-ceo-evan-spiegel-mocks-mark-zuckerberg-meta-copycat-features/))

**Tinder vs. Bumble:** In 2018, Match Group (Tinder's parent) sued Bumble for patent infringement, trade secret misappropriation, and trademark infringement -- claiming Bumble copied Tinder's "world-changing, card swipe-based, mutual opt-in premise." Bumble's response: **"We swipe left on you. We swipe left on your multiple attempts to buy us, copy us, and, now, to intimidate us."** The case eventually settled. ([Harvard JOLT](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/match-group-tinder-v-bumble-online-dating-company-which-owns-tinder-sues-dating-app-founders-by-tinders-co-founders), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2018/10/30/660006488/the-tinder-bumble-feud-dating-apps-fight-over-who-owns-the-swipe))

**The broader pattern:** TikTok's short-form video was copied by Instagram (Reels), YouTube (Shorts), and Snapchat (Spotlight). LinkedIn added Stories. Twitter added Fleets (then killed them). The entire social media industry operates on a copy-first model with effectively zero IP consequences.

### Lotus v. Borland (1995) -- The "Look and Feel" Precedent

In a critical early case, Lotus sued Borland for copying the menu hierarchy of Lotus 1-2-3 in its competing spreadsheet, Quattro Pro. No source code was copied -- only the names and organization of commands.

The First Circuit reversed the district court and ruled the menu hierarchy was an uncopyrightable **"method of operation."** The Supreme Court heard the case but split 4-4 (one justice recused), leaving the First Circuit's ruling in place but setting no national precedent. ([Wikipedia: Lotus v. Borland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotus_Dev._Corp._v._Borland_Int%27l,_Inc.))

**Why this matters:** Lotus v. Borland established (at least in the First Circuit) that the *organizational structure* of a software product -- how menus are arranged, how commands are named, how the interface is structured -- is not copyrightable. It's a "method of operation." This is the legal ancestor of the modern reality: you can copy someone's product concept, interaction model, and information architecture as long as you write your own code and make it look sufficiently different.

### The Smartphone Patent Thicket

The modern smartphone is covered by an estimated **250,000 active patents** -- representing one in six of all active U.S. patents. A smartphone requires technologies covered by patents from hundreds of different companies: LCD screens, antennas, Wi-Fi standards, processors, batteries, touch interfaces, cameras, audio codecs, compression algorithms, and on and on. ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/articles/20121017/10480520734/there-are-250000-active-patents-that-impact-s), [WIPO Magazine](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/the-sky-is-not-falling-navigating-the-smartphone-patent-thicket-55490))

New entrants face "royalty stacking" -- paying multiple licensing fees that raise production costs to the point where market entry becomes uneconomical. "If you don't bring any patents to the table, you essentially won't be allowed to enter the market." ([Yale Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/are-patent-thickets-smothering-innovation))

Companies like Apple, Samsung, Google, and Qualcomm maintain vast patent portfolios primarily for **defensive purposes** -- mutual assured destruction. The patents aren't for asserting rights; they're for cross-licensing negotiations. Google's $12.5 billion acquisition of Motorola in 2012 was primarily for its patent portfolio.

## VI. The AI Inflection Point

### The Protection Gap -- The Thesis

The most valuable things in product design -- interaction patterns, feature concepts, design decisions, product strategy, user experience innovations -- have **no effective legal protection**:

- **Copyright** protects the wrong thing (the specific code, not the idea behind it)
- **Utility patents** are too slow (3-5 years to obtain) and too expensive ($15K-$30K+) for fast-moving software, and **Alice** made software patents dramatically harder to get
- **Design patents** protect only specific ornamental appearances, not the underlying concept
- **Trade dress** requires the look to be distinctive and non-functional -- extremely narrow for software
- **Trade secrets** require keeping the feature secret, which is impossible for user-facing products

### Alice + AI = Perfect Storm

Two developments have converged catastrophically:

**Alice Corp. (2014)** made software patents dramatically harder to obtain and defend:
- 82.9% average invalidation rate for computer-implemented patents
- Section 101 rejections doubled
- Only 4 out of 27 software patents upheld by Federal Circuit in 2020
- The practical result: most software product innovations cannot be effectively patented

**Generative AI (2022-present)** made feature replication near-instant:
- Competitor sees your feature --> describes it to AI --> working implementation in hours
- The "moat" of development time and cost has collapsed
- What used to take a team months now takes a prompt and a weekend

Together: the legal tool that could protect product ideas (patents) has been weakened, while the threat it was supposed to guard against (copying) has been amplified by orders of magnitude.

### Can AI Be Named as an Inventor?

The **DABUS case** tested this globally. Dr. Stephen Thaler created an AI system called DABUS (Device for Autonomous Bootstrapping of Unified Sentience) and filed patent applications naming DABUS as the inventor in multiple jurisdictions. ([Wikipedia: DABUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DABUS))

**Results:**
- **United States (2022):** Federal Circuit ruled AI cannot be an inventor on a U.S. patent. Only natural persons can be inventors. ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/en/perspectives/2022/08/sorry-dabus-ai-cannot-be-an-inventor-on-a-us-patent))
- **United Kingdom (2023):** Supreme Court unanimously confirmed AI cannot be an "inventor" under the Patents Act. ([Womble Bond Dickinson](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/uk/insights/articles-and-briefings/supreme-court-confirms-ai-cannot-be-inventor-under-patents-act))
- **European Patent Office (2024):** Denied DABUS's European patent. ([Patently-O](https://patentlyo.com/patent/2024/12/denied-european-patent.html))
- **South Africa:** Granted the patent -- the only jurisdiction to do so, sparking significant debate.

### USPTO's Current Position on AI-Assisted Inventions (November 2025)

The USPTO released revised inventorship guidance in November 2025:
- Only **natural persons** may be named as inventors
- AI is treated as a "tool that assists" in the inventive process, analogous to "laboratory equipment, computer software, [or] research databases"
- Human inventors must "conceive the invention with a definite and permanent idea"
- Inventors may use AI to "provide services and generate ideas," but the human must be the actual inventor under traditional conception standards
- Prompts generally cannot be patented as standalone inventions

([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/subscription-center/2025/revised-inventorship-guidance-ai-assisted-inventions), [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/12/uspto-issues-revised-inventorship-guidance-for-ai-assisted-inventions))

**Why this matters:** If you use AI to generate a product feature, you (the human) can still be named as inventor *if* you contributed the inventive concept. But if the AI generated the idea and you merely prompted it -- the dominant pattern in spec-driven development -- the invention may not be patentable at all. This creates a paradox: the more AI contributes to the inventive process, the less protectable the result becomes.

### The Figma Lawsuit as a Case Study

**Khan v. Figma Inc. (November 2025):** A class action alleging Figma secretly trained AI models on millions of customer design files -- including layer properties, text, images, and proprietary business IP -- without adequate consent. ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/2025-11-20-1814))

**What makes this case unusual:**
- It's not a standard copyright case -- it alleges **trade secret misappropriation** and unauthorized data access
- The design files contain proprietary business IP that goes beyond copyrightable expression
- Figma's defense: "Our training is focused on general patterns -- not on customers' unique content, concepts and ideas"

**Figma's defense is the protection gap in microcosm.** "Patterns" and "general concepts" aren't protected by copyright. Figma is essentially saying: we extracted the thing that has value (patterns, approaches, design systems) while leaving the thing that's protected (the specific pixel arrangement). This is legally correct and practically devastating for designers.

### Absurd Patent Examples (For Color/Humor)

The patent system has produced some spectacularly ridiculous grants:

- **Method of swinging on a swing (2002):** A lawyer patented the method his young son used to swing sideways rather than back and forth -- an activity children have engaged in since the invention of the swing. ([Above the Law](https://abovethelaw.com/2017/10/8-of-my-favorite-stupid-patents/))
- **Exercising a cat with a laser pointer (1995):** A patent for the method of pointing a laser at a wall and watching a cat chase it.
- **IBM's out-of-office email (2017):** IBM obtained a patent for... an out-of-office email messaging system.
- **Crustless peanut butter and jelly sandwich:** Two men patented the sealed crustless PB&J. J.M. Smucker bought them out and named it the Uncrustable.

These examples serve a purpose beyond humor: they illustrate that the patent system's quality control has genuine failures. When you can patent swinging on a swing, the system's claim to protect genuine "inventions" becomes harder to take seriously.

## VII. The Protection Gap: Connecting Patent History to the AI Moment

### The Recurring Pattern

Every major reproduction technology has triggered the same cycle in patent/IP law:

| Era | Technology | IP Challenge | Resolution |
|-----|-----------|-------------|------------|
| 1474 | Manufacturing innovation | No systematic protection for inventions | Venetian Patent Statute |
| 1624 | Royal monopoly abuse | Patents conflated with political patronage | Statute of Monopolies |
| 1790s | Early industrial scale | How to evaluate inventions fairly | Jefferson's rigorous examination |
| 1850s | Complex manufactured goods | Multiple patents on single product | Sewing Machine Combination (patent pool) |
| 1870s-1900s | Telegraph, telephone, electricity | Race-to-file disputes, market monopolies | Patent warfare, antitrust |
| 1900s-1910s | Aviation | Patent thickets block development | Government-mandated patent pool |
| 1990s-2000s | Software, internet | Can you patent a business method? | State Street (yes) -> Alice (mostly no) |
| 2010s | Smartphones | 250,000 patents on one product | Cross-licensing, defensive portfolios |
| 2020s | Generative AI | Instant replication + weakened patents | **Unresolved** |

### What's Different This Time

In every previous era, the resolution followed a pattern: legal frameworks adapted to cover the new technology. But the AI era may be qualitatively different:

1. **Speed of replication has outpaced the patent system.** Patents take 3-5 years to obtain. AI replication takes hours. By the time you have a patent, the landscape has shifted entirely.

2. **The most valuable innovations aren't patentable.** Post-Alice, most software product innovations -- interaction patterns, feature concepts, UX approaches -- are considered "abstract ideas" that can't be patented. The things worth protecting fall in the gap between what patents cover (novel technical processes) and what they don't (the idea of swiping right to indicate preference).

3. **The disclosure requirement backfires.** The patent grand bargain requires you to publicly disclose how your invention works. In the AI era, disclosure + AI means a competitor can use your patent application as a blueprint to replicate your invention instantly. The 20-year monopoly was supposed to compensate for disclosure; when AI collapses the replication timeline, the bargain breaks down.

4. **Design patents protect the wrong layer.** A design patent protects *this specific visual treatment* of a swipe gesture. It doesn't protect *the concept of swiping to choose*. A competitor can take your UX concept, give it a different visual treatment, and avoid infringement entirely.

5. **Trade secrets don't work for user-facing features.** The moment users interact with your feature, it's public knowledge. Trade secrets protect processes that happen behind closed doors -- not the experience you deliver to customers.

### The Silicon Valley View

The tech industry has largely abandoned patents as a competitive strategy. As Google's John La Barre put it: "It's not that Silicon Valley hates the patent system. We just have an opinionated sense of what that means." ([IAM Media](https://www.iam-media.com/article/silicon-valley-isnt-anti-patent-insist-big-software-players-some-remain-be-convinced))

Patents in tech serve primarily **defensive** purposes -- building portfolios for cross-licensing negotiations and deterring lawsuits. They're shields, not swords. The actual competitive advantages -- speed, network effects, data, brand, embedded workflows -- are unrelated to IP law.

InfoWorld's take: "Save Silicon Valley -- abolish patents now." The argument: "Patents are supposed to reward innovation, but in the software industry, they are having the opposite effect." ([InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2613654/save-silicon-valley----abolish-patents-now.html))

## IX. Potential Article Angles (Building on the Copyright Research)

### Angle 1: The Grand Bargain Is Broken
Trace the patent grand bargain from Venice (1474) through AI (2026). The deal was: disclose your invention, get a temporary monopoly. But AI breaks both sides of the bargain. Disclosure + AI = instant replication (the monopoly is worthless). And the things worth protecting (interaction patterns, product concepts) aren't patentable anyway. The grand bargain assumed copying was hard and inventions were physical. Neither is true anymore.

### Angle 2: The Total Protection Gap
Combine the copyright research with the patent research to map the **complete landscape of what's NOT protected** in product design. Copyright doesn't protect ideas. Patents don't protect abstract software concepts. Design patents don't protect the *concept* behind a visual treatment. Trade dress doesn't protect functional elements. Trade secrets don't work for user-facing features. Map the gap between what has value (UX innovations, product strategy, interaction patterns) and what the law covers (almost none of it).

### Angle 3: History Repeats -- Until It Doesn't
Every technology disruption (printing press, player piano, VCR, internet, AI) triggered the same IP panic and eventual adaptation. But each time, something was genuinely lost before the system adapted. The question: is this time different? The Wright brothers story as cautionary tale -- patents can destroy the very innovation they're supposed to protect. Whitney's cotton gin as prologue -- the most valuable inventions are sometimes the hardest to protect.

### Angle 4: Speed Is the Only Moat
If IP law can't protect product ideas, what does? The answer from tech: speed, network effects, data, brand, embedded workflows. The article as practical guide for designers and product people: stop trying to protect your ideas and start trying to execute faster than anyone can copy them. The protection gap isn't a legal problem -- it's a strategic one.

### Angle 5: The 550-Year Arc
From Brunelleschi's sinking boat (1427) to Figma's AI training (2025): a 550-year history of trying to protect inventions. The tension between the inventor's desire for control and society's desire for access. How each era found a different balance -- and why the AI era may be the first time there's no balance to find.
