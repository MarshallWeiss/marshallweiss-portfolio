# Article Shape: The Grand Bargain Is Broken

## Working thesis
The patent system was built on a "grand bargain" — disclose your invention, get temporary protection. That bargain assumed replication was hard. AI makes replication trivial. The bargain collapses.

## Tone & Style
- Long-form journalistic — New Yorker / Atlantic feature
- NOT personal essay, NOT "day in the life of a designer"
- Academic-adjacent but readable, anecdote-driven
- History told through stories, not textbook summaries

## Structure (Draft)

### 1. The Grand Bargain (Opening)
- Start with the philosophical concept: society strikes a deal with inventors
- You reveal how your invention works (disclosure), we give you a temporary monopoly (protection)
- The assumption underneath: copying is hard, so the monopoly window matters
- Jefferson as first patent examiner, his candle metaphor — ideas naturally spread
- Brief sweep of origins (Venice 1474, Statute of Monopolies 1624, US Patent Act 1790)
- Keep this section tight — it's setup, not the story

### 2. The Cotton Gin Problem (Historical Parallel)
- Eli Whitney invents the cotton gin (1794)
- So simple anyone could copy it — and everyone did
- His patent was essentially worthless despite the invention being transformative
- The invention "so valuable as to be worthless to the inventor"
- **This is the thesis in miniature**: when copying is easy, protection fails
- Draw the line forward: what happened to Whitney is about to happen to everyone

### 3. Patents Meet Software (The Troubled History)
- Software patents are inherently weird — patenting abstract logic
- The arc: Diamond v. Diehr (1981, software becomes patentable) → State Street Bank (1998, floodgates open) → patent trolls emerge → Alice v. CLS Bank (2014, earthquake — ~83% of software patents invalidated)
- Alice essentially said: you can't patent an abstract idea just because it runs on a computer
- The patent troll phenomenon — NTP extracting $612M from BlackBerry without making anything
- The Wright Brothers parallel: innovators who became patent trolls, litigation so aggressive it left America with no military-ready aircraft by WWI
- **Key point**: even before AI, the patent system was already failing for software

### 4. Speed Was Always the Real Game
- How tech companies actually compete: speed, network effects, data, distribution — not patents
- Facebook copying Snapchat Stories and winning through distribution
- Everyone copying Tinder's swipe
- The "fast follower" was already the dominant strategy
- But there was still a *window* — copying took teams of engineers, weeks or months
- Apple v. Samsung ($533M for rounded rectangles) — design patents as a weapon but ultimately a sideshow

### 5. AI Closes the Window (The Inflection Point)
- AI compresses the replication cycle from weeks/months to hours
- The first-mover advantage shrinks to near-zero
- Scale stops being a copying advantage — a solo developer with AI can replicate what took a team of 10
- The Figma lawsuit as case study — the tool designers use was training on their work
- DABUS case: AI can't be an inventor — the more AI contributes, the less protectable the result
- **The protection gap**: copyright protects expression (wrong thing), patents are too slow and expensive, trade dress is too narrow — nothing covers product ideas, interaction patterns, design decisions

### 6. Where This Is Going (Ending)
- If patents don't protect software and AI makes copying instant, what does the competitive landscape actually look like?
- The moat shifts from "can you build it?" to ecosystem: brand, trust, community, distribution, data
- Possible trends: open-source-by-default (if you can't protect it, share it and build community), speed-of-iteration as the only differentiator, consolidation around platforms with network effects
- The "grand bargain" may need a new version — what would a patent system designed for AI look like? Or is the concept itself obsolete for software?
- Does innovation slow down when inventors can't capture value? Or speed up because ideas flow freely? (The Whitney question again)
- Callback to the cotton gin / grand bargain to close — the system was built for a world where copying was hard, and that world is gone

## Open Questions
- How much copyright/patent distinction to explain? Need enough for readers to understand but it shouldn't become a legal primer
- How design-specific to make it? The examples are tech-industry but the thesis is universal
- Length: this feels like 5,000-7,000 words — medium-long
- Section 6 needs more research on where the software industry is actually heading — what are companies doing now to compete if not patents?
