## I. Origins of Copyright (Pre-1710)

### The Ancient Roots

Copyright-like protections predate the modern era by millennia, though they took radically different forms:

- **500 B.C.E.**: Chefs in the Greek colony of Sybaris were granted year-long monopolies for creating particular culinary delights — one of the first known references to intellectual property protection. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/))

- **1st century B.C.E.**: Vitruvius is said to have revealed intellectual property theft during a literary contest in Alexandria. Serving as judge, he exposed poets who had stolen the words of others. The plagiarists were tried, convicted, and disgraced. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/))

- **1421**: Florence issued a patent to the architect Filippo Brunelleschi — the man who designed the cathedral dome — establishing "the rights of authors and inventors to the products of their intellectual efforts." ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/))

- **1474**: Venice's patent statute — 150 years ahead of England's — featured inventor rights recognition, infringement compensation, and term limits. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/))

### The Stationers' Company Monopoly (1403-1695)

**The key backstory to all modern copyright.**

The Worshipful Company of Stationers was formed in London in 1403 as a guild of scribes, illuminators, and bookbinders. In **1557**, Mary I granted them a royal charter that gave the Stationers an extraordinary deal: a complete monopoly on all printing in England, in exchange for serving as the Crown's private-sector censors. ([Wikipedia: Stationers' Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worshipful_Company_of_Stationers_and_Newspaper_Makers), [Copyright History: Stationers' Charter 1557](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_uk_1557))

**How it worked:**
- Once a Stationer registered a text in the company's register, no other member could publish it.
- This created the original "copy right" — literally, the right to copy. But it belonged to the **printer**, not the **author**.
- Authors had no rights whatsoever. A writer who sold a manuscript to a printer lost all control over it.
- The system served two masters: the government got censorship, and the printers got monopoly profits.

**The key insight for the article:** The very term "copyright" was born not to protect creators, but to protect the commercial interests of publishers and the censorship interests of the state. Authors were an afterthought. ([Wikipedia: History of Copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_copyright), [Copyright History Project](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_uk_1557))

### The Collapse of the Monopoly (1695)

In **1694**, Parliament refused to renew the Licensing of the Press Act (which had been the statutory foundation for the Stationers' power since 1662). The monopoly collapsed. Presses could now operate freely outside London. ([Wikipedia: Statute of Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Anne))

The Stationers were desperate to get their monopoly back. But they needed a new argument — "censorship is good" wasn't going to fly in a more liberal Parliament.

**Enter Daniel Defoe.**

### Daniel Defoe: The First Copyright Advocate (1704-1710)

Daniel Defoe — yes, the author of *Robinson Crusoe* — was arguably the first public advocate for author-centric copyright. In **1704**, he published "An Essay on the Regulation of the Press," arguing for statutory protection against "press-piracy." He framed the issue not as a guild privilege, but as a matter of **authorial rights** — the "scandalous and unjust Invasions of Property" perpetrated on writers. ([Copyright History: Defoe's Essay](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_uk_1704))

Defoe mounted a sustained press campaign through his *Review* periodical in advance of the Statute of Anne. He provided both the philosophical framing and, crucially, gave the Stationers a new rhetorical strategy: instead of arguing for their own monopoly, they could argue for **authors' rights** — and then, conveniently, continue to control the actual printing. ([Copyright History: Defoe's Essay](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_uk_1704))

On December 3, 1709, Defoe published in *A Review* a direct demand for "a Law in the present Parliament... for the Encouragement of Learning, Arts, and Industry, by securing the Property of Books to the Authors or Editors of them." Three days later, he submitted a draft text for the bill. ([Wikipedia: Statute of Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Anne))

### The Statute of Anne (1710)

**The world's first copyright statute.**

On April 5, 1710, Queen Anne granted royal assent to "An Act for the Encouragement of Learning, by Vesting the Copies of Printed Books in the Authors or Purchasers of such Copies, during the Times therein mentioned." ([Wikipedia: Statute of Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Anne), [Britannica: Statute of Anne](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Statute-of-Anne))

**Key features:**
- **Copyright vested in authors, not publishers** — for the first time in history.
- **14-year term**, renewable for another 14 years if the author was still alive.
- **Automatic reversion:** Regardless of what authors signed away to publishers, the second 14-year term would automatically return to them. This was a direct response to publishers' abuse of authors.
- **Public domain:** After the term expired, works belonged to the public. This was revolutionary — the concept of a **limited** monopoly, after which knowledge becomes free.

**The title itself tells the story:** The act was "for the Encouragement of Learning" — not for the enrichment of authors or publishers. Copyright was framed as a tool for the public good. The author's monopoly was a *means*, not an *end*. ([History of Information](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=3389), [Copyright History Commentary](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_uk_1710))

## II. Philosophical Foundations: Why Does Copyright Exist?

### 1. Locke's Labor Theory (Natural Rights)

**The argument:** John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1690) argued that individuals own the fruits of their labor. If you mix your labor with a commonly available resource, you create property. By extension, intellectual work — writing a book, composing music, creating art — is labor that produces property, and the creator has a natural right to it. ([Wikipedia: Labor Theory of Copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_theory_of_copyright), [Harvard Cyber Law](https://cyber.harvard.edu/IPCoop/88hugh.html))

**The nuance:** Locke included the famous "proviso" — your appropriation of resources is only legitimate if it leaves "enough and as good" for others, and you don't waste what you take. Applied to intellectual property, this means: your copyright is valid as long as it doesn't impoverish the commons of ideas. ([Oxford Academic: Lockean Copyright](https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/doi/10.1093/jla/laaa002/5836775))

**Why it matters for the article:** The Lockean framework is the strongest *pro-copyright* argument. It says: creators have a natural right to their work, regardless of what any statute says. This is the intuition most people have ("I made it, so it's mine"). It also contains its own limitation: copyright must not make the intellectual commons worse off.

### 2. The Utilitarian/Incentive Argument

**The argument:** Copyright exists not because creators have a natural right, but because society *benefits* from incentivizing creation. Without copyright, creators can't recoup their investment, so they'll create less. The monopoly is a deal: we give creators temporary control so they'll keep producing. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/))

**This is the dominant Anglo-American framework.** It's what the U.S. Constitution enshrines: Congress may grant copyrights "to promote the progress of science and useful arts." The purpose is public benefit, not author enrichment.

**Why it matters for the article:** The utilitarian argument contains the seeds of its own destruction in the AI age. If AI can create endlessly without economic incentive, the utilitarian case for copyright weakens. This is exactly Mark Lemley's argument: "We need copyright only if we think we won't get enough creation without it. That may no longer be a worry."

### 3. Hegel's Personality Theory

**The argument:** Works of authorship are extensions of the creator's personality. An artist's painting is not just a product — it's an expression of their inner self. Copyright protects this intimate connection between creator and creation. This framework emphasizes **moral rights** — the right to attribution, the right to prevent distortion of your work — over economic rights. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/))

**Why it matters for the article:** This is the strongest argument against AI extraction of style. Under the personality theory, your voice, your aesthetic, your approach aren't just commercial assets — they're extensions of your identity. AI that replicates your style is violating something deeper than a commercial monopoly.

### 4. Thomas Jefferson's Skepticism

Jefferson offered the most eloquent counterargument to copyright as natural right. In his famous 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson:

> "If nature has made any one thing less susceptible than all others of exclusive property, it is the action of the thinking power called an idea, which an individual may exclusively possess as long as he keeps it to himself; but the moment it is divulged, it forces itself into the possession of every one, and the receiver cannot dispossess himself of it."

And the candle metaphor:

> "He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me."

Jefferson acknowledged copyright as potentially useful policy — "Society may give an exclusive right to the profits arising from them, as an encouragement to men to pursue ideas which may produce utility" — but insisted it was a **pragmatic concession**, not a natural right. And even then, he noted: "other nations have thought that these monopolies produce more embarrassment than advantage to society." ([University of Chicago Press: Founders' Documents](https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/a1_8_8s12.html))

**Why it matters for the article:** Jefferson's candle metaphor is eerily relevant to AI. An AI model that "learns" from a copyrighted work is doing exactly what Jefferson described: receiving an idea without diminishing the original. The question is whether this analogy holds, or whether AI training is fundamentally different from human learning.

## III. The Battle of the Booksellers (1769-1774)

**The most dramatic episode in early copyright history.**

After the Statute of Anne's 14+14 year terms started expiring, London publishers (the "booksellers") claimed that they still held **perpetual common-law copyright** — that the Statute of Anne had merely *added* to their existing natural rights, not replaced them. This was, in effect, an attempt to maintain the old Stationers' monopoly under a new name. ([Wikipedia: Donaldson v Becket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaldson_v_Becket))

### Millar v. Taylor (1769)

The first round went to the publishers. The case concerned James Thomson's poem *The Seasons*, whose statutory copyright had expired. The court ruled 3-1 that authors possessed inherent common-law rights independent of statute — a **perpetual copyright**. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intellectual-property/), [Copyright History: Donaldson v. Beckett](https://www.copyrighthistory.com/donaldson.html))

### Donaldson v. Beckett (1774) — The Decisive Battle

Scottish bookseller Alexander Donaldson — whose business was built on reprinting books whose statutory terms had expired — challenged the perpetual copyright claim. The case went to the House of Lords.

**The arguments were extraordinary:**

- Attorney General Thurlow dismissed literary property as too "abstruse and chimerical" to define.
- Sir John Dalrymple delivered a two-and-a-half-hour argument spanning metaphysics, chemistry, and history. He compared ideas to water: "If I take water from the ocean it is mine, if I pour it back it is mine no longer."
- Baron Eyre branded exclusive copyright claims a monopoly and noted that Lord Hardwick's papers contained a large "Q" (for doubt) marking his skepticism about perpetual author's rights.
- Lord Chief Justice De Grey warned that perpetual copyright would let authors "demand exorbitant prices or withhold reprints indefinitely."
- Lord Camden's concluding remarks were scathing, dismissing the publishers' historical precedents as "that heterogeneous heap of rubbish."

**The ruling (6-5):** Authors possessed **no perpetual common-law copyright**. The Statute of Anne created a statutory right for a limited term only. After that, works entered the public domain. ([Copyright History: Donaldson v. Beckett](https://www.copyrighthistory.com/donaldson.html), [UCL Law Faculty](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/laws/events/2024/feb/recording-battle-booksellers-250th-anniversary-donaldson-v-beckett))

**Why it matters for the article:** This 1774 case established the principle that copyright is a **limited statutory creation**, not a natural right. It's the foundation for the public domain. And the pattern — publishers claiming broader rights, society pushing back — repeats in every era, including the current AI dispute.

## IV. The Industrial Revolution Era (1800s)

### Mass Production Transforms the Calculus

The 19th century brought technologies that made reproduction cheaper and faster than ever:

- **Steam-powered rotary printing presses** (1843) allowed printing on an industrial scale — ultimately millions of copies per day, compared to the hundreds possible with hand presses.
- **Stereotyping** lowered the cost of reprints dramatically.
- **Lithography** (invented c. 1796, commercialized in the 1800s) made it possible to produce enormous editions of prints from a single drawing on limestone.
- **Photography** (1830s-1840s) created the first truly mechanical reproduction of visual reality.

Each of these forced copyright law to expand. The subject matter of copyright grew through the century to include: musical compositions, plays, engravings, sculpture, and photographs. ([Franklin & Marshall Library Guide](https://library.fandm.edu/c.php?g=750623&p=5400204), [EH.net Economic History](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/an-economic-history-of-copyright-in-europe-and-the-united-states/))

### Copyright Terms Begin Their Expansion

This is where the ratchet begins — and never stops:

| Year | Jurisdiction | Term |
|------|-------------|------|
| 1710 | England (Statute of Anne) | 14 years + 14-year renewal |
| 1790 | U.S. (Copyright Act) | 14 years + 14-year renewal |
| 1831 | U.S. | 28 years + 14-year renewal |
| 1842 | U.K. | Author's lifetime + 7 years |
| 1909 | U.S. | 28 years + 28-year renewal (56 total) |
| 1976 | U.S. | Life + 50 years |
| 1998 | U.S. (Sonny Bono Act) | Life + 70 years (95 years for corporate works) |

([ARL Copyright Timeline](https://www.arl.org/copyright-timeline/), [Copyright Term Extension Act: Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Term_Extension_Act))

As Lawrence Lessig noted: the original term of 14 years has ballooned to effectively 200 years. "In the past thirty years the average term has tripled." ([Econlib Interview](https://www.econlib.org/library/Columns/y2003/Lessigcopyright.html))

### Charles Dickens and the Piracy Problem (1842)

**One of the great copyright anecdotes.**

Dickens arrived in America on January 22, 1842, already a rock star — because pirated copies of *The Pickwick Papers* and *Oliver Twist* had flooded the American market at cheap prices. The books that made him famous in America were the same books that made him no money there. He called himself "the greatest loser alive by the present law." ([Foley Hoag](https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/making-your-mark-blog/2017/january/charles-dickens-and-copyright-law-five-things-you-should-know/), [Victorian Web](https://victorianweb.org/authors/dickens/pva/pva75.html))

At a dinner held in his honor in Boston, Dickens dared to criticize his hosts: "I would beg leave to whisper in your ears two words, International Copyrights." Americans were staggered. They expected gratitude; instead, this young British visitor was calling them pirates. The press turned hostile. ([Creative Law Center](https://creativelawcenter.com/dickens-american-copyright/), [American Heritage: "Copywrong"](https://www.americanheritage.com/copywrong-short-history-literary-piracy))

**Why it matters for the article:** The pattern — a new technology or market making copying trivially easy, creators screaming for protection, the beneficiaries of the copying outraged at being called pirates — is identical to the AI dispute. In 1842, Americans genuinely believed they had a right to cheap books. Today, AI companies genuinely believe they have a right to train on everything.

### Burrow-Giles v. Sarony (1884) — Can a Machine Be an Author?

**A case that could have been decided yesterday.**

Napoleon Sarony was a celebrity photographer who took a famous portrait of Oscar Wilde in 1882 — "Oscar Wilde No. 18." The Burrow-Giles Lithographic Company made unauthorized lithograph copies. Sarony sued. ([Wikipedia: Burrow-Giles v. Sarony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burrow-Giles_Lithographic_Co._v._Sarony), [Columbia Law Scholarship](https://scholarship.law.columbia.edu/books/297/))

**The defense's argument:** Photographs are mere mechanical reproductions — a machine captures an image, not an author. Therefore, photographs aren't "writings" by "authors" under the Constitution, and cannot be copyrighted. Congress overstepped in extending copyright to photography.

**The Court's answer:** Photographs CAN be copyrightable, but only when they reflect the creative choices of the photographer — "posing the said Oscar Wilde in front of the camera, selecting and arranging the costume, draperies, and other various accessories... arranging the subject so as to present graceful outlines, arranging and disposing the light and shade, suggesting and evoking the desired expression." ([Justia: Burrow-Giles v. Sarony](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/111/53/))

**Why it matters for the article:** This is the direct ancestor of the AI copyrightability question. In 1884, the question was: when a machine (camera) is involved in creation, how much human authorship is required? In 2025, the U.S. Copyright Office answered the same question for AI: works can be protected "only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements." The framework is 141 years old.

### The Berne Convention (1886)

**The first international copyright treaty.**

By the 1880s, the problem Dickens had complained about in 1842 had only gotten worse. Authors' works were pirated across borders with no recourse.

**Victor Hugo** — then the most famous writer in the world — led the charge. In **1878**, under Hugo's presidency, internationally known writers formed the International Literary Association in Paris. At its 1883 meeting in Berne (Hugo presiding), the group produced a draft treaty. ([Wikipedia: Berne Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berne_Convention), [History of Information](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=1278))

On **September 9, 1886**, ten nations signed the Berne Convention: Belgium, France, Germany, Haiti, Italy, Liberia, Spain, Switzerland, Tunisia, and the United Kingdom.

**Key innovations:**
- **Automatic protection:** Copyright exists the moment a work is "fixed" (written or recorded). No registration required.
- **National treatment:** Foreign authors get the same rights as domestic authors in every signatory nation.
- **Moral rights (droit d'auteur):** The Convention was heavily influenced by the French tradition, which emphasized not just economic rights but the author's moral connection to their work — the right to attribution and the right to prevent distortion.

**The U.S. didn't join until 1989** — over a century later — because it required registration as a condition of copyright, which violated Berne's automatic-protection principle. ([Wikipedia: Berne Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berne_Convention), [ALAI History](https://www.alai.org/en/information/history.html))

## V. The Idea-Expression Dichotomy and Fair Use Origins

### Baker v. Selden (1879) — Ideas Can't Be Copyrighted

Charles Selden developed a novel bookkeeping system and published it in a book. William Baker produced similar account books using the same system but with a different arrangement of columns. Selden sued.

The Supreme Court ruled that copyright protects only the **expression** (the particular way Selden described his system), not the **idea** (the bookkeeping system itself). "Copyright gives no exclusive right to the art disclosed; protection is given only to the expression of the idea — not the idea itself." ([Wikipedia: Baker v. Selden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker_v._Selden), [Berkeley Law](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/center-article/the-story-of-baker-v-selden-sharpening-the-distinction-between-authorship-and-invention/))

This became the **idea-expression dichotomy** — codified in the Copyright Act of 1976 as Section 102(b). It's one of the two most fundamental doctrines in copyright law.

**Why it matters for the article:** The idea-expression dichotomy is precisely the gap AI exploits. AI extracts ideas, patterns, and styles — which aren't protected — and discards the specific expression, which is. The law was designed for a world where the threat was copying the expression. AI makes the expression irrelevant.

### Folsom v. Marsh (1841) — The Birth of Fair Use

Reverend Charles Wentworth Upham copied 353 pages from a 12-volume biography of George Washington to produce his own two-volume work. Judge Joseph Story, ruling on the case, articulated what would become the four factors of fair use:

Courts must consider "the nature and objects of the selections made, the quantity and value of the materials used, and the degree in which the use may prejudice the sale, or diminish the profits, or supersede the objects, of the original work." ([Wikipedia: Folsom v. Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folsom_v._Marsh), [Copyright History](https://www.copyrighthistory.org/cam/tools/request/showRecord.php?id=commentary_us_1841))

These factors were eventually codified in the Copyright Act of 1976 as Section 107. Over 180 years later, they're still the framework courts apply — including in every AI copyright case today.

## VI. Technology Panics: A Recurring Pattern

### John Philip Sousa and "The Menace of Mechanical Music" (1906)

In June 1906, the most famous American composer of the era testified before Congress. Player pianos and phonographs were booming, and Sousa was terrified. He told Congress: **"These talking machines are going to ruin the artistic development of music in this country."** ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/john-philip-sousa-feared-menace-mechanical-music-180967063/), [Library of Congress Blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/now-see-hear/2020/05/sousa-and-the-talking-machine/))

He published an essay in *Appleton's Magazine* entitled "The Menace of Mechanical Music," with extraordinary rhetorical flourishes:

- On the quality of mechanical music: "as like real art as the marble statue of Eve is like her beautiful, living, breathing daughters"
- On amateur musicians disappearing: "the amateur disappears entirely, and with him a host of vocal and instrumental teachers"
- On children: they would "become simply human phonographs — without soul or expression"
- On national health: "Then what of the national throat? Will it not weaken? What of the national chest? Will it not shrink?"
- On romance: he conjured the image of a "man and maiden in a light canoe under the summer moon upon an Adirondack lake with a gramophone caroling love songs"
- His warning: "Let us not hamper it with a machine that tells the story day by day, without variation, without soul"

([MIT OCW: Full text](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/21m-380-music-and-technology-contemporary-history-and-aesthetics-fall-2009/18ab3aba9fe7aa1502a55cd049333659_MIT21M_380F09_read02_sousa.pdf), [ExplorePAHistory](https://explorepahistory.com/odocument.php?docId=1-4-1A1))

**Why it matters for the article:** Sousa's fears are an almost perfect template for today's AI anxieties. Replace "talking machines" with "generative AI," "amateur musicians" with "independent designers," and "without soul or expression" with "visual elevator music." The pattern is uncanny.

### White-Smith v. Apollo (1908) — When Machines Can't "Read"

The player piano case. White-Smith Music Publishing sued the Apollo Company, which manufactured piano rolls. The question: are piano rolls "copies" of copyrighted sheet music?

The Supreme Court ruled **no** — because piano rolls weren't human-readable. Since "hardly anyone could perceive (read) music by looking at a roll of paper with holes in it," the rolls were merely parts of a machine, not copies of a composition. ([Wikipedia: White-Smith v. Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-Smith_Music_Publishing_Co._v._Apollo_Co.))

**Congress fixed this within a year** with the Copyright Act of 1909, which established that mechanical reproductions counted as copies. But — in a brilliant compromise — Congress simultaneously created the **compulsory license**: anyone could record anyone's song, provided they paid a small standardized fee (originally $0.02 per copy). This prevented monopolies while compensating creators. ([Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2014/05/white-smith-music-case-a-terrible-1908-supreme-court-decision-on-player-pianos.html))

**Why it matters for the article:** The piano roll case is the most direct historical precedent for AI training. The core question — does a machine that processes copyrighted material in a non-human-readable format make a "copy"? — is structurally identical to the question of whether AI training on copyrighted texts constitutes copying. And the *solution* — compulsory licensing — is structurally identical to the "learnright" proposals for AI.

### Mark Twain's White Suit (1906)

On December 7, 1906, 71-year-old Mark Twain appeared before Congress's Joint Committee on Patents at the Library of Congress to argue for extending copyright terms. In spite of the "keen December wind," he appeared in a cream-colored flannel suit that shocked the room. His friend William Dean Howells described it: "It was a magnificent coup, and he dearly loved a coup." ([Library of Congress Blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2017/12/this-day-in-history-mark-twain-debuts-iconic-style-at-library-of-congress/))

Twain argued for copyright lasting an author's lifetime plus 50 years. With characteristic wit: **"I think that will satisfy any reasonable author, because it will take care of his children. Let the grandchildren take care of themselves."**

Twain had long suffered from piracy, particularly in Canada and Britain. Though the 1906 bill failed, his proposed term (life + 50) eventually became law — in 1976, seventy years after his testimony. ([Library of Congress Blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2017/12/this-day-in-history-mark-twain-debuts-iconic-style-at-library-of-congress/))

### Jack Valenti and the VCR (1982)

The MPAA president's testimony to Congress in 1982 is the most quoted copyright panic statement in history:

> "I say to you that the VCR is to the American film producer and the American public as the Boston strangler is to the woman home alone."

Valenti called the VCR an "avalanche" and a "tidal wave" and said it would make the film industry "bleed and bleed and hemorrhage." He campaigned for a tax on blank videotapes, arguing that home taping would "bankrupt the studios." ([Wikipedia: Jack Valenti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Valenti), [Slashdot](https://news.slashdot.org/story/02/05/31/1622232/valentis-boston-strangler-testimony))

**What actually happened:** The home video market became one of the most lucrative revenue streams in film history. The technology that was supposed to destroy the industry became its biggest profit center.

## VII. The Digital Era (1984-2021)

### Sony v. Universal (1984) — The Betamax Case

Universal Studios and Walt Disney sued Sony, arguing that the Betamax VCR enabled copyright infringement. In a razor-thin **5-4 decision**, the Supreme Court ruled that:

- **Time-shifting** (recording a TV show to watch later) was fair use.
- A technology manufacturer couldn't be held liable for contributory infringement if the device was "capable of commercially significant noninfringing uses."

This "staple article of commerce" doctrine became the foundation for every subsequent technology-vs-copyright dispute. ([Wikipedia: Sony v. Universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_Corp._of_America_v._Universal_City_Studios,_Inc.), [Marquette Law](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/2010/11/the-most-important-supreme-court-case-in-copyright-law-sony-corp-v-universal-city-studios-1984/))

**Why it matters for the article:** If the vote had gone 5-4 the other way, VCRs would have been illegal. The history of home entertainment — and by extension, the internet — would have been radically different. Copyright cases often come down to one vote.

### Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) — Transformative Use

2 Live Crew parodied Roy Orbison's "Oh, Pretty Woman" without permission. In a **unanimous 9-0 decision**, the Supreme Court established the concept of "transformative use" — uses that add "new expression, meaning, or message" to the original are more likely to be fair use, even if commercial. The more transformative the new work, the less significant other factors like commercialism become. ([Wikipedia: Campbell v. Acuff-Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell_v._Acuff-Rose_Music,_Inc.))

This decision has been cited in over 500 subsequent court opinions. It's the doctrinal foundation for Judge Alsup's ruling in *Bartz v. Anthropic* that AI training is "quintessentially transformative."

### The DMCA (1998) — The Internet's Copyright Framework

The Digital Millennium Copyright Act, signed by President Clinton on October 28, 1998, was Congress's attempt to bring copyright into the internet age. It implemented the WIPO Copyright Treaty of 1996 and established two key frameworks: ([Wikipedia: DMCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Millennium_Copyright_Act), [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/digital_millennium_copyright_act))

**1. Safe Harbor Provisions (Section 512):** Online service providers aren't liable for user-uploaded infringing content, as long as they follow a "notice and takedown" process. Without these protections, YouTube, Facebook, and essentially all user-generated content platforms would be legally impossible. ([Congress.gov: CRS Report](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF11478))

**2. Anti-circumvention provisions:** It's illegal to circumvent DRM (digital rights management) technologies, even for otherwise legal purposes. Richard Stallman had warned about this in his 1997 dystopian short story "The Right to Read," which envisioned a future where sharing books was a crime. The DMCA's anti-circumvention provisions were a step in that direction. ([GNU: The Right to Read](https://www.gnu.org/philosophy/right-to-read.en.html))

### The Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act (1998)

In the same year as the DMCA, Congress extended copyright by 20 years — to life + 70 for individual authors and 95 years for corporate works. The act was commonly called the **"Mickey Mouse Protection Act"** because it came just before *Steamboat Willie* (1928) would have entered the public domain. Disney had lobbied for the extension since 1990. ([Wikipedia: CTEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_Term_Extension_Act))

Lawrence Lessig challenged the act in *Eldred v. Ashcroft* (2003), arguing that perpetual retroactive extensions were unconstitutional. The Supreme Court disagreed, 7-2. ([Supreme Court: Eldred v. Ashcroft](https://www.copyright.gov/pr/eldred.html))

Lessig later reflected that from the founding 14-year term to the current system, copyright had expanded to the point where "an entire generation of films that would have begun to fall into the public domain would remain under copyright lock for another twenty years." He argued that Walt Disney himself built his empire by adapting public domain stories — *Snow White*, *Cinderella*, *Pinocchio* — and then used copyright extension to prevent anyone else from doing the same. ([Wikipedia: Free Culture (book)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Culture_(book)))

### Napster (1999-2001) — Digital Reproduction Breaks Everything

In 1999, 18-year-old Shawn Fanning created Napster — a peer-to-peer file sharing service that let anyone share MP3 music files for free. At its peak, 80 million registered users were trading music. The music industry's assumptions about distribution — that reproduction costs money, that distribution requires physical media — were shattered overnight. ([Wikipedia: A&M Records v. Napster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A&M_Records,_Inc._v._Napster,_Inc.))

In **A&M Records v. Napster** (2001), the Ninth Circuit rejected Napster's fair use defense. File sharing was not "sampling" or "space-shifting" — it was mass infringement. Napster shut down in July 2001, paid $26 million in settlements, and filed for bankruptcy. ([Justia: A&M Records v. Napster](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/239/1004/636120/))

But the genie was out of the bottle. Napster's successors (Kazaa, Grokster, LimeWire, BitTorrent) proved that digital reproduction couldn't be stopped by killing one service.

### MGM v. Grokster (2005) — The Inducement Doctrine

The Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Grokster — a post-Napster peer-to-peer service — could be held liable for **inducing** copyright infringement. The key evidence: StreamCast (maker of Morpheus) had deliberately targeted former Napster users, advertising to them specifically. Grokster paid $50 million in damages and shut down. ([Wikipedia: MGM v. Grokster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MGM_Studios,_Inc._v._Grokster,_Ltd.), [Cornell LII: Grokster](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/04-480.ZS.html))

The Court established the **inducement test**: anyone who distributes a device with the intent of promoting its use to infringe copyright is liable, regardless of the device's other capabilities.

### Authors Guild v. Google (2015) — Mass Digitization as Fair Use

Google digitized tens of millions of books from research library collections — without permission — to create a full-text searchable index (Google Books). The Authors Guild sued.

The Second Circuit unanimously ruled for Google. **Mass digitization for search purposes was fair use.** The use was "highly transformative" because Google wasn't reproducing books for reading — it was creating a search index that served a fundamentally different purpose. The commercial nature of Google's operation didn't matter. ([Justia: Authors Guild v. Google](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/13-4829/13-4829-2015-10-16.html), [Wikipedia: Authors Guild v. Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authors_Guild,_Inc._v._Google,_Inc.))

**Why it matters for the article:** This is the most direct legal precedent for AI training. Google copied entire books to create a search tool — and courts called it fair use because the purpose was transformative. AI companies copying books to train language models are making essentially the same argument. Judge Alsup in *Bartz v. Anthropic* explicitly built on this reasoning.

### Google v. Oracle (2021) — Software and Fair Use

Google copied 11,500 lines of Java API declarations to build Android. Oracle sued. In a **6-2 decision**, the Supreme Court ruled Google's use was fair, bypassing the question of whether APIs are copyrightable at all. The Court cited "rapidly changing technological, economic, and business-related circumstances" as a reason to rest the holding solely on fair use. ([Wikipedia: Google v. Oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_LLC_v._Oracle_America,_Inc.), [Congress.gov: CRS Report](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10597))

### Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) — Narrowing Transformative Use

Just two years after the Google v. Oracle expansion of fair use, the Supreme Court narrowed it. Andy Warhol's silkscreen series based on Lynn Goldsmith's photograph of Prince was NOT fair use, 7-2. The Court held that creating a work with a "new meaning or message" isn't sufficient for transformative use — the **purpose** of the new work must be different from the original. Both Goldsmith's photo and Warhol's print served the same purpose: illustrating a magazine article about Prince. ([Wikipedia: Warhol v. Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Warhol_Foundation_for_the_Visual_Arts,_Inc._v._Goldsmith))

**Why it matters for the article:** Warhol v. Goldsmith creates tension with the Google Books and Google v. Oracle precedents. If AI generates content that serves the same purpose as the training data — a text that competes with the original book, an image that competes with the original photograph — Warhol v. Goldsmith suggests that's not transformative, even if the AI added "new meaning."

## VIII. The Bridge to AI: What Was Already Broken

Before AI entered the picture, copyright law was already strained in several critical ways:

### 1. The Orphan Works Crisis

Millions of copyrighted works whose rights holders cannot be identified or located. Libraries, museums, and archives are afraid to digitize them because of "draconian penalties in copyright law." The U.S. Copyright Office's 2015 report documented the problem extensively. Projects are "changed or abandoned due to fears that some of the material may have been published and registered." The increase in orphan works results from two factors: copyright terms have been lengthened, and copyright is automatically conferred without registration or renewal. ([U.S. Copyright Office: 2015 Report](https://www.copyright.gov/orphan/reports/orphan-works2015.pdf))

### 2. The Permission Culture

For mass digitization involving millions of copyrighted works, "obtaining permission is essentially impossible" — not because rights holders can't be identified, but because of "the sheer number of individual permissions required." The transaction costs of copyright compliance exceed the value of most uses. Lawrence Lessig argued this constituted a "permission culture" that was strangling innovation. ([U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/orphan/reports/orphan-works2015.pdf), [Econlib: Lessig Interview](https://www.econlib.org/library/Columns/y2003/Lessigcopyright.html))

### 3. The Term Extension Problem

From 14 years in 1710 to life + 70 years today. Lessig: the original term has ballooned from 14 years to "closer to two hundred years." Works that would have entered the public domain decades ago remain locked up. Walt Disney built an empire on public domain stories, then lobbied to extend copyright so nobody could do the same with Disney's works. ([Wikipedia: Free Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Culture_(book)))

### 4. The Fair Use Uncertainty

Fair use has always been a defense, not a right — meaning you have to litigate to prove you have it. As the EFF noted, copyright holders regularly used the threat of litigation to suppress legitimate uses, including using DMCA takedowns to remove a Harvard law professor's lecture that used brief clips for educational purposes. ([EFF: Search Engines, AI, and Fair Use](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/01/search-engines-ai-and-long-fight-over-fair-use))

### 5. The Idea-Expression Gap Was Already Showing

The doctrine that copyright protects expression but not ideas, established in Baker v. Selden (1879), was already under pressure. In a world of digital remixing, sampling, and adaptation, the line between copying an idea and copying expression was increasingly blurry — long before AI made it irrelevant.

### 6. The Pattern of Technology Panics

As the EFF documented: "Long before generative AI, copyright holders warned that new technologies for reading and analyzing information would destroy creativity." Photocopiers, VCRs, search engines — each was called an "infringement machine." Each time, courts found that the technology's benefits outweighed the copying involved. Each time, the predicted apocalypse didn't arrive. ([EFF: Search Engines, AI, and Fair Use](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/01/search-engines-ai-and-long-fight-over-fair-use))

But the pattern also shows something else: **each technology did cause real harm to some creators, even as it benefited society overall.** Player pianos did destroy some musicians' livelihoods. Home taping did reduce record sales. Napster did devastate the music industry before streaming emerged. The question with AI is whether the pattern holds — eventual adaptation — or whether this time the disruption is qualitatively different.

## IX. The Recurring Pattern: A Summary

Every major reproduction technology has triggered the same cycle:

1. **New technology** makes copying cheaper/easier
2. **Creators and incumbents** declare it will destroy their industry
3. **Courts** struggle to apply old frameworks to new realities
4. **The technology wins** — some version of it survives legal challenge
5. **Copyright law adapts** — usually by expanding to cover the new medium, creating licensing frameworks, or establishing new fair use boundaries
6. **Some creators are harmed, but culture is enriched**

| Era | Technology | "This will destroy us" | Legal response | Outcome |
|-----|-----------|----------------------|----------------|---------|
| 1710s | Printing press at scale | Stationers' monopoly threatened | Statute of Anne | Authors get rights, limited terms |
| 1840s | Steam printing, international piracy | Dickens: "the greatest loser alive" | Berne Convention (1886) | International protection |
| 1880s | Photography | "Machines can't be authors" | Burrow-Giles v. Sarony (1884) | Photos are copyrightable |
| 1900s | Player pianos, phonographs | Sousa: "menace of mechanical music" | Copyright Act of 1909, compulsory license | Mechanical reproduction covered |
| 1980s | VCR | Valenti: "Boston Strangler" | Sony v. Universal (1984) | Time-shifting = fair use |
| 1990s-2000s | Internet/P2P | Music industry devastated | DMCA (1998), Napster (2001), Grokster (2005) | Safe harbors, inducement doctrine |
| 2010s | Mass digitization | "They're copying everything!" | Authors Guild v. Google (2015) | Search indexing = fair use |
| 2020s | Generative AI | "They're stealing our work!" | Bartz v. Anthropic, NYT v. OpenAI, etc. | **Still being decided** |

**The question the article must answer:** Is AI just the latest entry in this table — another technology that will force copyright to adapt, as it always has? Or is it qualitatively different — a technology that doesn't just copy expression but absorbs and reproduces the *ideas, styles, and patterns* that copyright was never designed to protect?
