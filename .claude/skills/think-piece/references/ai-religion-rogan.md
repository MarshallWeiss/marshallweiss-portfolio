Hark! A sign of the End Times. No, not the Four Horsemen, nor a black sun, nor the resurrection of the dead. The omen that the rapture is upon us is none other than artificial intelligence.

At least, such is the prophecy that has issued forth from the great syncretic dorm room of American culture: Joe Rogan's mind. In November, the most popular podcaster in the country suggested that the next holy manger might be located not in the Middle East, but inside a mainframe instead.

"Jesus was born out of a virgin mother; what's more virgin than a computer?" he mused on the "American Alchemy" podcast. He added: "If Jesus does return — even if Jesus was a physical person in the past — you don't think that he could return as artificial intelligence?"

After all, he noted: "It reads your mind, and it loves you."

Mr. Rogan's speculation followed months of conversation about A.I. and the End Times. Also in November, Paul Kingsnorth, a writer and critic of modernity, appeared on the New York Times Opinion podcast "Interesting Times" and told the host, Ross Douthat, that "the constant progress of the technological system" — rapid advances in A.I. — would "lead to the rise of beings which are very Antichrist-like": a false prophet imitating the Second Coming.

Mr. Kingsnorth's augury came after a June appearance on the same show by Peter Thiel, the tech investor and right-wing political activist, who made an opposite prediction: that a figure promising safety and salvation from those same rapid changes could soon arise, and that this figure would be the Antichrist, leading us astray from technological deliverance.

Mr. Thiel and Mr. Kingsnorth are both idiosyncratic figures who for years have been thinking their way toward apocalyptic conclusions. And they are both thinking alongside a tech industry that for at least 20 years — since the futurist Ray Kurzweil popularized the idea of the technological singularity, in which A.I. surpasses human intelligence — has concerned itself with the last days of humankind as we know it.

But these men are not merely eccentrics. They reflect a new atmosphere charged with religious feeling. More and more, when it comes to our relationships with A.I. and the complex algorithms that shape so much of our modern subjectivity, we have slipped into the language and habits of mind we normally reserve for deities. And even people who do not make an explicit connection between A.I. and religion engage a kind of religious mode around the new technology.

This turn comes in the context of an inescapable — one might say biblical — amount of prophesying about A.I. since 2022, when OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public. That large language model and its impressive successors have made it slightly harder to dismiss the idea that we are on the verge of a technological revolution, in which machines with greater capabilities than humans will meaningfully reshape human experience.

"It's a natural response to either be afraid of what we're being told, or to embrace radical, almost messianic hope as an alternative to being terrified," said Greg Epstein, the humanist chaplain at Harvard University and the author of the 2024 book "Tech Agnostic: How Technology Became the World's Most Powerful Religion, and Why It Desperately Needs a Reformation."

Much of the general public's sense of the supposedly transcendent promise of A.I. comes from its interactions with chatbots like ChatGPT, which seem to know everything. (And for many, "A.I." and "chatbot" are used interchangeably.) Just as important are our everyday engagements with A.I. through the personalization algorithms that drive modern social media. These have become so specific, and at times so uncanny, that they can at times seem to contain a spark of something human, or beyond human — divine.

It can be hard to look backward in the face of technology that promises unprecedented transformation. But the human impulse to find awe and assign magical properties to the unknown is very old indeed. No, we aren't ancients seeing in every rainstorm and fire the awesome deeds of capricious deities. For one thing, we know where A.I. comes from — it comes from us. Still, a similar impulse has been activated. And it's one that can be put to many uses.

'A Pre-Enlightenment Way of Thinking'

Scientists have long understood that we anthropomorphize computers, turn them into characters. In much the same way that our brains fill in visual gaps when our vision is partly blocked (a phenomenon one Victorian physicist attributed to "God, the 'Divine Artificer'"), we naturally take a few strands of computer-generated text and weave them into an entire being.

In 1985, Lucy Suchman, an anthropologist at Xerox's fabled Palo Alto Research Center, memorably described the phenomenon as "the tendency to ascribe full intelligence on the basis of partial evidence."

"As soon as computational artifacts demonstrate some evidence of recognizably human abilities, in other words, we are inclined to endow them with the rest," Ms. Suchman wrote. But A.I. chatbots provide much more than partial evidence of intelligence. Colloquial and always improving, they are not just begging to be personified, they personify themselves by design.

And we can't help it, because the human bias toward anthropomorphization is an ancient one. According to the University of Michigan anthropologist Webb Keane, author of "Animals, Robots, Gods: Adventures in the Moral Imagination," when we map human traits onto computers, we're participating in a long tradition that includes the Delphic oracle, spirit mediums and I Ching yarrow stalks. The Delphic oracle was a woman who was thought to be in direct contact with the god Apollo — the word of a deity given human form. Just as chatbots seem to mediate between a greater intelligence via a human voice, so was the oracle a literal medium.

When humans create "something like us that has the possibility of knowing beyond what we can know," Professor Keane said, "it is a technology for superior insights."

The algorithms that govern A.I. are indeed technologies for superior insight: guides to and messengers from the unfathomably vast digital universe that humans have spent the past four decades creating. At times, A.I. chatbots are disembodied, like the God of Abraham, communicating with us only through text. At other times, they appear as digital avatars.

Avi Schiffmann, the creator of the much-maligned Friend pendant, a wearable chatbot., recognizes this power. In an interview with The Atlantic, Mr. Schiffman said that "the closest relationship this is equivalent to is talking to a God."

The creators of A.I.-powered apps that allow believers to "text with Jesus" — or similar "godbots" for Muslims and Hindus — seem to have had the same insight.

"What unites the gods of every spiritual tradition is that they are capable of communicating with us," Meghan O'Gieblyn, author of the 2021 book "God, Human, Animal, Machine: Technology, Metaphor and the Search for Meaning," wrote in an email. "Now we are faced with another invisible, nonhuman form of intelligence that communicates linguistically with us. The slippage into describing A.I. religiously feels almost inevitable."

To get some sense of the prophetic texture of the chatbot-user relationship, think of the most extreme example: "chatbot psychosis," in which people are driven to madness through their conversations with an A.I. It recalls nothing so much as the lunatic who claims to be directly receiving God's inscrutable instructions.

Because like all gods, this technology is inscrutable. Some of the most popular chatbots, like ChatGPT, are considered black boxes — technologies whose inner workings are so complex that even the people who created them can't easily explain them. Sometimes they produce outputs that are thrillingly specific or helpful, creating the sense in the human user of being preternaturally known.

So too do the A.I.-backed personalization algorithms that drive so much of contemporary media consumption. They produce uncanny results, seeming at times to overhear our conversations and predict our desires.

"What if the TikTok algorithm knows me better than I know myself?" asked a GQ Australia article.

There is even a popular TikTok format dedicated to the sometimes eerie intimacy of the app's "For You" page.

"When your for you page shows you a video of a thought you had recently and never even said out loud," reads the text in one video.

"When the fyp is mentioning things I only said in my brain," reads another.

Like all memes, these contain a dose of irony. But they also reflect the extent to which the algorithm can seem to transmit from a mysterious dimension, beyond mere human understanding.

"It's almost like we're returning to a pre-Enlightenment way of thinking," Ms. O'Gieblyn said. "One in which we have to take a decision or a prediction on faith, as pure revelation."

'Scrolling Is a Digital Prayer'

In 1841, the German philosopher Ludwig Feuerbach made the revolutionary argument that God is the "outward projection of man's inner nature"; 60 years later, the French sociologist Émile Durkheim suggested that "God is society, writ large." If humans make God in their image, it follows that to understand a society, we would do well to understand the characteristics of its deities.

No one, except perhaps Mr. Rogan, would argue that A.I. chatbots have divine origins; they are trained in our image, even if we don't understand them. And there are important ways in which our use of chatbots differs from other forms of divination.

For one, it is mostly individual. While Durkheim wrote of "collective effervescence," the exhilaration of participating in a communal act of religious worship, our interactions with A.I. are atomized. There is technically a church dedicated to worshiping A.I. — the engineer Anthony Levandowski's outlandish Way of the Future. But communication with A.I. is mostly done alone.

In a May essay, "You're Literally Worshiping Your Phone," the linguist Adam Aleksic wrote on his popular Substack that "micro-religious" attitudes have permeated contemporary social media use. Awe at the way the algorithm seems to know us, Mr. Aleksic asserts, isn't so much different from the commonplace belief that God works in mysterious ways. Both responses reduce forces that seem to exceed human understanding into a graspable shorthand. More than that, Mr. Aleksic argues, interacting with the algorithm has come to resemble a religious ritual.

"Scrolling is a digital prayer," he said in an interview. "You assume it knows a piece of you. You are offering your attention and in exchange you get something."

As anyone who has ever prayed in a house of worship or to God in bed at night will know, prayers are not always answered. But the act itself gives comfort and hope. Here lies an important difference between Mr. Aleksic's digital prayer, which is a guaranteed transaction, and traditional religious prayer.

The chatbot prayer is always met immediately with an answer. And the answer comes from an unctuous prophet: An analysis published this year found that A.I. chatbots are 50 percent more sycophantic than humans. Calibrated to maximize engagement, the major chatbots flatter their users and reflect back to them their worldview. The A.I.-driven personalization algorithm creates playlists for you and only you.

According to Professor Keane, the anthropologist of religion, the relationship between users and chatbots is defined by "narcissistic individualism," with A.I. incapable of delivering the "hard truths" of a traditional prophet or oracle. "I am the only one who gets to initiate the conversation," Professor Keane said. "It is there for me, and it serves me."

And why might the corporations that make chatbots want to lock users in with the easy answers of an obsequious deity? "Companies are trying to get trillions of dollars of investment," said Mr. Epstein, the humanist chaplain. "Religion is a system of thinking and action that can get people to overcome what might otherwise be profound skepticism to build something at enormous scale that may not serve their own direct interests but might serve a greater whole that is purportedly transcendent."

And A.I. boosters do promise transcendence. Tech titans including Sam Altman and Marc Andreessen have predicted an Edenic future powered by godlike algorithms, in which gains in productivity obviate the need for jobs and humans enjoy what one report by Mr. Andreesen's venture capital firm A16 called an "Era of Abundance." To be sure, these are self-interested promises from people who stand to increase their vast fortunes.

"The more we credit it with divine powers, the more we reinforce the power of the corporations that sell it," Professor Keane said.

Perhaps, along with the swell of religious feeling toward technology, we are also seeing another form of deification in real time: of the small handful of men and women who profit every time we bow our heads toward a device.
